# Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models (Wei, Wang, Schuurmans, Bosma, Ichter, Xia, Chi, Le, Zhou, Google Brain, 2022 — arXiv 2201.11903)

## The story in one paragraph
Big language models could answer many facts, but they flopped on grade-school math and tricky commonsense puzzles, and making them bigger barely helped. Nine Google researchers noticed that people solve hard problems by thinking aloud step by step, so they tried giving the model a few worked examples that show the intermediate reasoning, not just the final answer. With only eight such examples in the prompt and no extra training, the largest models suddenly learned to generate their own step-by-step chains before answering. A 540-billion-parameter PaLM with chain-of-thought set a new record on the GSM8K math benchmark and generalized to new puzzles, proving that prompting could unlock reasoning that was already latent inside large models.

## What problem was hurting before this paper
Standard few-shot prompting gave the model input-output pairs like question then answer, which works for simple recall but fails when a problem needs multiple hops. On datasets like GSM8K math word problems, accuracy stayed low and flat as models grew, because the model tried to jump straight to the answer and made arithmetic or logic slips along the way. The alternative was expensive: hire experts to write thousands of detailed rationales and fine-tune a separate model for each task, as in Cobbe and colleagues work on verifiers, which costs time and loses generality. Neuro-symbolic systems that output formal equations had similar brittleness and needed special training. The field needed a cheap, general way to get multi-step reasoning out of a single frozen checkpoint that could serve many tasks.

## The core idea, explained simply (with an analogy)
The core idea is to show your work. Imagine a math teacher who only accepts final numbers versus one who asks pupils to write each line of working. The second class learns to catch errors and gets partial credit for good process. Chain-of-thought prompting does the same for language models. Instead of prompting with question and final answer only, you prompt with question, a short natural-language derivation, and then the answer. At test time the model imitates that style: it writes its own intermediate sentences, which act like scratch paper that spreads computation across more tokens and makes the path checkable. Because the scratch paper is in plain English, the same trick works for math, commonsense, date puzzles, sports judgments, and even robot plans without changing the model.

## How it actually works (step by step: architecture, training objective, inference — explain each piece in words; explain key equations in plain language)
There is no new architecture and no training in this paper, which is the point. The authors take off-the-shelf autoregressive transformers, including LaMDA up to 137 billion parameters, GPT-3 variants up to 175 billion including text-davinci-002, PaLM at 8, 62, and 540 billion, plus UL2 20B and Codex, and they only change the prompt text.

For arithmetic they hand-wrote eight exemplars, each formatted as a question followed by a few sentences of reasoning and a final sentence ending with the answer is followed by the number. One famous example reasons that Roger starts with 5 balls, 2 cans of 3 is 6 more, and 5 plus 6 is 11. For the multiple-choice AQuA algebra set they used four training examples with similar rationales. For commonsense sets like CSQA and StrategyQA they sampled training questions and wrote chains that eliminate wrong options or state implicit facts, for example noting that a pear density is about 0.6 grams per cubic centimeter so it floats. For symbolic tasks like last-letter concatenation they wrote chains that name each last letter and then join them, and for coin flip they wrote chains that count flips and apply the even-means-heads, odd-means-tails rule.

At inference time the test question is appended after these frozen exemplars, and the model generates greedily until it stops. There is no gradient update and no calculator during generation, though the authors later rescore equations with an external Python evaluator as an analysis. In plain language, the model is doing next-token prediction as always, but because the prompt established a pattern of thinking before answering, the most likely continuation is a coherent chain followed by a correct final answer. The paper explains that this helps in three ways: it decomposes a hard task into easier steps, it gives the model more tokens of compute for harder problems, and it creates an interpretable window where humans can see where reasoning went wrong. Ablations prove the steps must come before the answer and must be meaningful language, not just dots or post-hoc explanations.

## Key results and numbers from the paper (with the actual scores/tables described in words)
Gains were striking and grew with scale and difficulty. On GSM8K grade-school math, PaLM 540B jumped from about 17.9 percent with standard prompting to about 56.9 percent with chain-of-thought, and to about 58.6 percent with an external calculator, beating the prior fine-tuned GPT-3 with verifier at about 55 percent. GPT-3 175B jumped from about 15.6 to about 46.9 percent, and Codex from about 19.7 to about 63.1 percent. On SVAMP the PaLM gain was from about 69.4 to about 79.0 percent, on MAWPS from about 79.2 to about 93.3 percent, and on ASDiv from about 72.1 to about 73.9 percent, while on the multiple-choice AQuA set PaLM rose from about 25.2 to about 35.8 percent. Easy one-step MAWPS subsets barely moved because they were already above 90 percent.

Commonsense also improved. With PaLM 540B, StrategyQA rose from about 68.6 to about 77.8 percent, beating the prior best of 69.4, date understanding from about 49.0 to about 65.3 percent, sports understanding from about 80.5 to about 95.4 percent, beating an unaided sports fan at 84 percent, SayCan robot planning from about 80.8 to about 91.7 percent, and CSQA from about 78.1 to about 79.9 percent. Symbolic tasks showed near-perfect in-domain solving and strong length generalization: last-letter concatenation of two-word names rose from single digits to about 99.4 percent for PaLM 540B, and it still reached about 94.8 percent on three-word and 63 percent on four-word names never shown in prompts. Coin flip reached 100 percent in-domain and about 90 percent on longer out-of-domain sequences. Ablations showed equation-only, dots-only for variable compute, and reasoning-after-answer all performed near baseline on GSM8K, while different annotators, concise styles, and random GSM8K exemplars all still beat baseline by large margins.

## Why this paper matters (what later work it unlocked — name the specific later papers among the 15 where relevant)
This paper matters because it revealed reasoning as an emergent ability that appears only past roughly 100 billion parameters, changing how everyone prompts large models. It showed that a frozen checkpoint could rival task-specific fine-tuning, which made prompting the default interface for complex work. Among the 15 papers, it builds on paper 03 on GPT-3 few-shot learning by upgrading bare answers to reasoned answers, and it was tested on paper 08 InstructGPT variants, showing that instruction following and reasoning reinforce each other. It directly unlocked paper 15 on DeepSeek-R1, which industrializes long chains of thought with reinforcement learning and self-consistency, and it foreshadowed efficient reasoning efforts like paper 11 on LoRA and paper 12 on FlashAttention that make long reasoning traces affordable, as well as paper 13 on LLaMA 2, whose chat models are routinely evaluated with chain-of-thought prompts.

## Honest limitations
Chain-of-thought only helps large models. Smaller models under about 10 billion parameters often produce fluent but illogical chains and actually do worse than standard prompting. Even large models are not guaranteed to reason correctly: manual review found that among wrong GSM8K answers, about 46 percent were nearly right with a calculator slip or missing step, while about 54 percent had major semantic or coherence errors, and correct answers can occasionally come from bad logic, especially on multiple-choice tasks. Annotation still requires human care, long chains cost more tokens and latency, factuality is not ensured, and serving a 500-billion-parameter reasoner is expensive for real products, motivating later work on distilling reasoning into smaller models.

## Fun trivia and history (from your web search)
The first author Jason Wei was a Google Brain researcher who also led work on instruction tuning and emergent abilities, and the chain-of-thought paper became so famous that Google CEO Sundar Pichai demonstrated it live at Google I/O 2022. The authors stressed that no model was fine-tuned while writing the paper, which surprised reviewers used to training. Within months the community spun the idea into self-consistency, which votes over many chains, least-to-most prompting, tree-of-thought and graph-of-thought, and the zero-shot variant that simply adds let's think step by step. Today every reasoning model from OpenAI o1 to DeepSeek-R1 traces its lineage to those eight handwritten math examples that taught giants to think aloud.

## Key terms explained (glossary of 5-8 terms)
Chain of thought means a series of intermediate natural-language steps that lead to a final answer, like showing working in a math exam. Few-shot prompting means giving a model a handful of examples in its input so it learns the task pattern without weight updates. Emergent ability means a skill that is absent in small models and appears suddenly past a scale threshold, here around 100 billion parameters. GSM8K means a benchmark of about 1319 grade-school math word problems that require two to eight steps to solve. Standard prompting means showing only questions and answers, while chain-of-thought prompting adds the reasoning in between. Self-consistency means sampling many different chains and taking the most common final answer to improve reliability. Length generalization means handling longer test problems than those shown in prompts, such as concatenating four letters after seeing only two.

## Connections (which earlier paper it builds on, which later paper builds on it)
This paper builds on paper 03 on GPT-3, which introduced in-context few-shot learning but showed flat scaling on reasoning, and on paper 05-style work on math datasets plus paper 02 on BERT-era pretraining that made large models possible. It was evaluated with GPT-3, LaMDA, PaLM, and paper 08 InstructGPT models, linking instruction following to reasoning. Among later papers, it is the conceptual parent of paper 15 on DeepSeek-R1, which turns manual chains into learned long reasoning with reinforcement learning, and it is routinely combined with paper 11 on LoRA for cheap reasoning adapters, paper 12 on FlashAttention for long reasoning contexts, paper 13 on LLaMA 2 for open chat reasoning, and paper 10 on Chinchilla thinking about how much compute reasoning deserves.
