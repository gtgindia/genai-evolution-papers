# Attention Is All You Need (Transformer) — Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser, Polosukhin, Google, 2017 — arXiv:1706.03762

## The story in one paragraph

This paper introduces a new neural network architecture called the Transformer that changed how computers handle language. Before this paper, the best translation systems read sentences one word at a time using loops called recurrences, which made training slow and made it hard to remember connections across long sentences. The authors show that you can throw away those loops entirely and rely only on a mechanism called attention, which lets every word look directly at every other word in one step. With this simple idea, plus several clever engineering details, they train much faster and beat all previous translation scores on standard English to German and English to French tests. The same model also works surprisingly well on English grammar parsing, which hints that the idea is general and not just a translation trick.

## What problem was hurting before this paper

Before the Transformer, sequence models were dominated by recurrent neural networks, especially long short-term memory networks and gated recurrent networks, and by encoder-decoder systems for translation. These models generate hidden states step by step along the positions of the sentence, so the computation for word number ten cannot start until word number nine is finished. This sequential nature makes parallelization almost impossible within a single example, and memory limits make it hard to compensate by using huge batches, especially for long sentences.

Attention mechanisms already existed and helped connect the encoder and decoder, so a decoder could look back at relevant source words no matter how far away they were. However, in almost all cases attention was used together with a recurrent network, so the sequential bottleneck remained. Convolutional alternatives like ByteNet, ConvS2S, and Extended Neural GPU could compute in parallel, but they connected distant words only through many stacked layers, which made long-range dependencies harder to learn. Training the best systems took very long and cost a great deal of computing power, and quality was plateauing.

## The core idea, explained simply (with an analogy)

The core idea is to let every position in a sentence directly talk to every other position through attention, with no recurrence and no convolution at all. Each word asks a question about what it needs, compares that question to labels on all other words, and then takes a weighted average of their meanings.

A helpful everyday analogy is a classroom discussion. In the old recurrent classroom, students could only whisper a message down the row, one student at a time, so the last student heard a blurry version of what the first student said. In the Transformer classroom, every student can instantly turn around, look at every other student, decide who is most relevant right now, and listen more to those voices. Multi-head attention is like having eight different listening strategies at once, so one head might listen for who did what to whom, another might listen for time expressions, and another might listen for descriptive details, and then they combine their notes.

Because all of these lookups happen in parallel with matrix math, training becomes much faster. Because any two words are connected in a single step, long-distance relationships like matching a verb with a faraway phrase become much easier to learn.

## How it actually works (step by step: architecture, training objective, inference — explain each piece in words; if there is a famous equation like scaled dot-product attention, explain what each part means in plain language)

The model follows the familiar encoder-decoder shape. The encoder reads the input sentence and turns it into a sequence of rich vectors, and the decoder generates the output sentence one token at a time, always allowed to look at what it already produced and at the full encoder memory. Generation is autoregressive, which means that to predict the next word you feed in all previous words.

The encoder is a stack of six identical layers. Each layer has two parts. The first part is multi-head self-attention, where every position attends to all positions in the previous layer. The second part is a position-wise feed-forward network applied separately to each position. Around each part there is a residual connection plus layer normalization, which in plain words means you add the input back to the output to keep signals flowing and then normalize to keep training stable. All layers use a model size of five hundred and twelve, so embeddings and sub-layer outputs share that width.

The decoder is also a stack of six identical layers. Each decoder layer has three parts. The bottom part is masked self-attention over already generated outputs, the middle part is encoder-decoder attention over the encoder memory, and the top part is the same feed-forward network. Masking means that when predicting position number five, the model is forbidden from peeking at positions six and beyond, which is enforced by setting illegal connections to negative infinity before the softmax step.

The famous equation is scaled dot-product attention. In words, you have three ingredients called queries, keys, and values. A query is like a question from the current word, a key is like a label on another word, and a value is like the content that other word offers. You compute a similarity score by taking the dot product of the query with every key, which simply means you multiply matching numbers and add them up, so large scores mean high relevance. You divide by the square root of the key dimension, which keeps the numbers from exploding when vectors are long. Then you apply softmax, which turns the scores into positive weights that sum to one, like percentages of attention. Finally you take a weighted sum of the values using those weights, so important words contribute more to the output.

The paper explains why scaling matters with a small statistics story. If query and key components are independent random numbers with mean zero and variance one, their dot product has mean zero and variance equal to the dimension. For large dimensions the dot products become very large in magnitude, which pushes softmax into regions with tiny gradients where learning stalls. Dividing by the square root of the dimension brings the variance back to one and restores healthy learning. Dot-product attention is also much faster than older additive attention because it can use highly optimized matrix multiplication.

Multi-head attention repeats this process eight times in parallel with different learned projections. Instead of one attention with full five hundred and twelve dimensions, the model projects queries, keys, and values eight times down to sixty-four dimensions each, runs attention eight times, concatenates the eight sixty-four dimensional results, and projects once more back to five hundred and twelve dimensions. The total cost stays similar to single-head attention, but the model can jointly attend to different kinds of relationships in different subspaces. One head in the paper clearly tracks long-distance verb phrases like making something more difficult, while other heads track pronouns like its pointing back to law, which the appendix visualizes beautifully.

There are three uses of attention in the model. Encoder-decoder attention lets every decoder position look at all encoder positions, which mimics classic translation alignment. Encoder self-attention lets every encoder position look at all encoder positions in the previous layer. Decoder self-attention lets every decoder position look only at earlier decoder positions including itself, to preserve the left-to-right generation rule.

After attention, each position goes through a feed-forward network with two linear steps and a ReLU in between. The inner layer expands to two thousand and forty-eight dimensions and then contracts back to five hundred and twelve. You can also think of this as two convolutions with kernel size one, because the same transformation is applied identically at every position but with different parameters in each layer.

Because there is no recurrence and no convolution, the model would otherwise have no sense of word order. The authors add positional encodings to the input embeddings at the bottom of both stacks. They use sine and cosine functions of different frequencies, where even dimensions use sine and odd dimensions use cosine of the position divided by powers of ten thousand. The wavelengths form a geometric progression from two pi to ten thousand times two pi. The intuition is that for any fixed offset, the encoding of position plus offset can be expressed as a linear function of the encoding of the original position, which should help the model learn relative positions. Learned positional embeddings work almost identically in their tests, but the sinusoidal version may extrapolate better to longer sentences than seen in training. Embeddings themselves are learned vectors of size five hundred and twelve, scaled by the square root of that size, and the same weight matrix is shared between input embeddings, output embeddings, and the final softmax layer.

Training uses the standard WMT 2014 English to German data with about four point five million sentence pairs and a shared byte-pair vocabulary of about thirty-seven thousand tokens, plus the larger WMT 2014 English to French data with thirty-six million sentences and a thirty-two thousand word-piece vocabulary. Batches contain roughly twenty-five thousand source tokens and twenty-five thousand target tokens grouped by similar length. The optimizer is Adam with beta one equal to zero point nine, beta two equal to zero point nine eight, and epsilon equal to ten to the minus nine. The learning rate rises linearly for the first four thousand warmup steps and then decays proportionally to the inverse square root of the step number, scaled by the inverse square root of the model size. Regularization includes residual dropout at rate zero point one, embedding dropout, and label smoothing with value zero point one, which makes the model less overconfident and improves BLEU even though perplexity gets slightly worse.

At inference time the authors use beam search with beam size four and length penalty alpha equal to zero point six, with maximum output length set to input length plus fifty but with early stopping when possible. For base models they average the last five checkpoints written at ten minute intervals, and for big models they average the last twenty checkpoints.

## Key results and numbers from the paper (with the actual scores/tables described in words)

On WMT 2014 English to German, the big Transformer reaches twenty-eight point four BLEU, which beats the best previous models including ensembles by more than two BLEU points. The base model alone reaches twenty-seven point three BLEU and already beats all previously published models and ensembles. On WMT 2014 English to French, the big model reaches forty-one point eight BLEU in the main table, described as forty-one point zero in one paragraph of the text, which sets a new single-model record at less than one quarter of the training cost of the previous best. The base model reaches thirty-eight point one BLEU on English to French.

Training cost is dramatically lower. The base model trains for one hundred thousand steps or about twelve hours on eight NVIDIA P100 graphics cards, with each step taking about zero point four seconds. The big model trains for three hundred thousand steps or about three point five days, with each step taking about one second. The authors estimate floating point operations by multiplying training time, number of graphics cards, and sustained capacity per card, and show orders of magnitude savings compared to GNMT plus reinforcement learning, ConvS2S, and mixture of experts systems.

Model variations on the English to German development set newstest 2013 tell a clear story. One attention head is zero point nine BLEU worse than eight heads, but too many heads like thirty-two also hurts. Reducing the key dimension hurts quality, which suggests compatibility is not trivial. Bigger feed-forward sizes and bigger model dimensions help, with the big configuration of six layers, model size one thousand and twenty-four, feed-forward size four thousand and ninety-six, and sixteen heads reaching twenty-six point four BLEU on the development set with two hundred and thirteen million parameters. Dropout is very helpful for avoiding overfitting, and sinusoidal versus learned positional encodings give nearly identical results around twenty-five point seven to twenty-five point eight BLEU.

On English constituency parsing with the Wall Street Journal portion of the Penn Treebank, a four-layer Transformer with model size one thousand and twenty-four reaches ninety-one point three F1 when trained only on forty thousand Wall Street Journal sentences, which already beats the Berkeley parser and all recurrent sequence models except recurrent neural network grammars. With semi-supervised training on about seventeen million high-confidence and Berkeley parser sentences, it reaches ninety-two point seven F1 and beats all previous models except multi-task and generative variants that reach ninety-three point zero and ninety-three point three. This is remarkable because recurrent sequence models had previously failed in the small-data regime.

## Why this paper matters (what later work it unlocked — name the specific later papers among the 15 where relevant)

This paper is the foundation for almost everything else in the set of fifteen papers. It replaces recurrence with stacked self-attention and establishes the encoder-decoder Transformer, the training recipe, and the way to think about path length and parallelization.

The BERT paper, which is number two in the set, takes the Transformer encoder directly, stacks twelve or twenty-four layers of bidirectional self-attention, and pre-trains with masked language modeling. The GPT-3 few-shot paper, which is number three, takes the Transformer decoder side with left-to-right masking and scales it to one hundred and seventy-five billion parameters to show in-context learning. The retrieval-augmented generation paper, which is number four, wraps Transformer generators with a retriever. The DDPM diffusion paper, which is number five, and the latent diffusion paper, which is number seven, use attention-based U-Nets and Transformer blocks for image generation, a direction the authors explicitly predict when they say they plan to handle images, audio, and video. The CLIP paper, which is number six, uses Transformer text and vision encoders trained contrastively. The InstructGPT paper, which is number eight, fine-tunes a GPT-3 style Transformer with human feedback. The chain-of-thought paper, which is number nine, elicits reasoning by prompting the same large Transformer decoders. The Chinchilla paper, which is number ten, revisits how to scale these Transformers optimally. The LoRA paper, which is number eleven, adapts Transformer query and value matrices efficiently. The FlashAttention paper, which is number twelve, makes the attention computation itself faster and memory efficient. The LLaMA-2 paper, which is number thirteen, the DPO paper, which is number fourteen, and the DeepSeek-R1 paper, which is number fifteen, all train and align modern Transformer decoders.

## Honest limitations

The paper itself is honest that self-attention costs quadratically in sequence length, which means very long documents, high-resolution images, audio, and video remain expensive, and the authors suggest restricted local attention as future work. Averaging attention-weighted positions reduces effective resolution, which multi-head attention only partly fixes. The sinusoidal positional encoding is a hypothesis rather than a proven solution for extrapolation, and learned embeddings do just as well in their tests. Label smoothing helps BLEU but hurts perplexity, which shows the model becomes more unsure in a way that is useful but not fully understood. Parsing results still fall short of the very best task-specific grammars, and translation evaluation is limited to two language pairs and to BLEU scores, which do not capture all aspects of quality.

## Fun trivia and history (from your web search)

The title is a playful reference to the Beatles song All You Need Is Love. One author, Jakob Uszkoreit, suspected that attention without recurrence would be enough for translation, hence attention is all you need. The name Transformer was chosen simply because Uszkoreit liked the sound of the word. An early internal design document was titled Transformers: Iterative Self-Attention and Processing for Various Tasks and included an illustration of six characters from the Transformers franchise, and the team called itself Team Transformer.

The author list has an asterisk for equal contribution and states that listing order is random, with a footnote that credits Jakob for the initial idea, Ashish Vaswani with Illia Polosukhin for the first models, Noam Shazeer for scaled dot-product and multi-head attention and the position representation, Niki Parmar and Llion Jones for code and variants, and Lukasz Kaiser and Aidan Gomez for the tensor2tensor library. The collaboration famously started from a hallway conversation in early 2017 at Google Mountain View between Vaswani and Uszkoreit about improving machine translation, which Shazeer overheard and joined with enthusiasm. Even Uszkoreit's father Hans, a well-known computational linguist, was reportedly skeptical at first.

Reception history is extraordinary. Presented at NeurIPS 2017 in Long Beach, the paper became one of the most cited papers in artificial intelligence history, with well over one hundred thousand citations within a few years. Strikingly, all eight authors eventually left Google to join other companies or found startups in the generative AI boom, including Cohere, Character AI, and other influential labs. At a 2024 Nvidia GTC reunion, seven of the eight authors joined Jensen Huang on stage and joked that the world now needs something better than Transformers.

## Key terms explained (glossary of 5-8 terms)

Sequence transduction means converting one sequence into another, such as translating an English sentence into a German sentence or parsing words into a grammar tree.

Encoder-decoder architecture means a two-part design where the encoder reads the whole input into memory vectors and the decoder writes the output step by step while looking at that memory.

Self-attention means an attention mechanism where queries, keys, and values all come from the same sequence, so each position learns from other positions in the same sentence.

Scaled dot-product attention means the specific formula that compares queries and keys with dot products, divides by the square root of the dimension to keep gradients healthy, applies softmax to get weights, and mixes values accordingly.

Multi-head attention means running several smaller attentions in parallel with different learned projections, then concatenating the results, so the model can capture different kinds of relationships at once.

Positional encoding means extra signals added to embeddings to tell the model about word order, using sine and cosine waves of different frequencies in this paper.

Autoregressive generation means producing outputs one token at a time, where each new token can only depend on previously generated tokens and not on future ones.

BLEU score means an automatic metric for translation quality that compares machine output to human reference translations using matching word sequences, where higher means closer to human references.

## Connections (which earlier paper it builds on, which later paper builds on it)

This paper builds on Bahdanau attention for neural machine translation, on the original sequence-to-sequence learning with recurrent networks, and on early self-attention work for reading comprehension, summarization, entailment, and memory networks. It explicitly compares itself to Extended Neural GPU, ByteNet, ConvS2S, and sparsely gated mixture of experts as alternative ways to reduce sequential computation.

Almost every later paper in the fifteen builds on it. BERT, which is number two, builds on the encoder stack. GPT-3, which is number three, builds on the decoder stack with causal masking. Retrieval-augmented generation, CLIP, latent diffusion, InstructGPT, chain-of-thought, Chinchilla, LoRA, FlashAttention, LLaMA-2, DPO, and DeepSeek-R1 all assume the Transformer as their backbone and then add retrieval, contrastive learning, diffusion, human feedback, prompting, scaling laws, efficiency, or reasoning on top.
