# Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models (Chinchilla) — Hoffmann, Borgeaud, Mensch et al., DeepMind, 2022 — arXiv:2203.15556

## The story in one paragraph

This paper asks a very practical question about how to spend a fixed amount of computer power when training a large language model. The authors trained more than four hundred small to medium models, ranging from seventy million to more than sixteen billion parameters, on five billion to more than four hundred billion tokens. From all of those runs they learned a simple rule for how to split extra computer power between making the model bigger and feeding it more text. They then proved the rule by training a new seventy-billion-parameter model called Chinchilla on one point four trillion tokens. Chinchilla used the same training computer budget as the much bigger two-hundred-and-eighty-billion-parameter Gopher model, yet Chinchilla beat Gopher, GPT-3, Jurassic-1, and the five-hundred-and-thirty-billion-parameter Megatron-Turing NLG on almost every test.

## What problem was hurting before this paper

Before Chinchilla, the field was following the scaling advice from Kaplan and colleagues from the year 2020. That advice said that when you get ten times more computer power, you should make the model about five and a half times bigger but only increase the training words by about one point eight times. In practice this meant that almost every big model was trained on roughly three hundred billion tokens, no matter how big it was. LaMDA had one hundred and thirty-seven billion parameters on one hundred and sixty-eight billion tokens, GPT-3 had one hundred and seventy-five billion parameters on three hundred billion tokens, Jurassic-1 had one hundred and seventy-eight billion parameters on three hundred billion tokens, Gopher had two hundred and eighty billion parameters on three hundred billion tokens, and Megatron-Turing NLG had five hundred and thirty billion parameters on two hundred and seventy billion tokens. The paper shows that all of these giant models were under-trained. They were like very large students who had only read a few textbooks, so they wasted their size because they had not seen enough examples to learn well.

## The core idea, explained simply (with an analogy)

The core idea is that model size and training words should grow together in equal steps. If you double the model size, you should also double the number of training tokens. If you get ten times more computer power, you should make the model about three times bigger and also feed it about three times more words.

A helpful analogy is cooking for a party. The model size is the size of your kitchen crew, and the training tokens are the ingredients you buy. Kaplan was telling people to keep hiring more cooks while buying almost the same amount of food. The result was a crowded kitchen with cooks standing around with nothing to chop. Chinchilla says that you should hire more cooks and buy more food together, so every cook stays busy and the meal actually gets better. The paper even gives a memorable rule of thumb that later became famous as the Chinchilla rule, which is about twenty training tokens for every single parameter in the model.

## How it actually works (step by step: method, math explained in words, experiments — explain each piece in words; explain key equations in plain language)

The paper wants to find the best model size, which it calls N, and the best number of training tokens, which it calls D, for any fixed computer budget, which it calls C. Computer budget is measured in FLOPs, which means floating-point operations, or simply how many math operations you can afford. The training loss, called L, measures how surprised the model is by real text, and lower loss means better learning.

The central equation is an optimization goal. In words it says to choose N and D so that the loss L of N and D is as small as possible, while keeping the total FLOPs equal to C. Since FLOPs are roughly six times N times D, you cannot increase both without limit. You must trade them against each other.

To solve this trade-off, the authors used three separate approaches, and all three agreed.

The first approach fixed the model size and varied the training time. They took families from seventy million up to ten billion parameters, and trained each one for four different lengths of time, with the longest run sixteen times longer than the shortest. For each run they smoothed the loss curve and drew a continuous line from FLOPs to loss. Then at fifteen hundred different FLOP values they asked which model size gave the lowest loss. They fitted a power law through those best points. A power law simply means a straight line on a log-log plot, which describes how the best size grows steadily with more computer power. This approach found that the best size grows like C to the power zero point five zero, and the best data also grows like C to the power zero point five zero. In plain words, both grow with the square root of computer power, which means they grow equally.

The second approach fixed the computer budget and varied the model size. These are called IsoFLOP curves, which means equal-FLOP curves. For nine different budgets from six times ten to the eighteen up to three times ten to the twenty-one FLOPs, they trained many models where bigger models got fewer tokens so that the total FLOPs stayed the same. When they plotted final loss against model size, each budget showed a clear valley shape, like a bowl with one lowest point. That lowest point is the best size for that budget. They fitted a parabola through each bowl to find the bottom precisely. Again they fitted power laws and found exponents of zero point four nine for size and zero point five one for data, which is essentially equal scaling.

The third approach fitted a single math formula to all of the results. The formula says that the predicted loss equals E plus A divided by N to the power alpha plus B divided by D to the power beta. In words, E is the unavoidable confusion of natural text itself, like the entropy or randomness that even a perfect writer cannot remove. The second term says that a model with only N knobs cannot perfectly imitate ideal language, and this gap shrinks as N grows. The third term says that even a good model is under-trained if it has only seen D words, and this gap shrinks as D grows. They fitted A, B, E, alpha, and beta using a robust fitting method called Huber loss with an optimizer called L-BFGS, trying many starting points to avoid bad local answers. The fitted values were E equals one point six nine, A equals four hundred and six point four, B equals four hundred and ten point seven, alpha equals zero point three four, and beta equals zero point two eight. From this formula they could solve for the best N and D with pencil and paper, and they got exponents of zero point four six and zero point five four, which again means nearly equal scaling.

A very important experimental detail was the learning-rate schedule. The learning rate controls how big each learning step is, and a cosine schedule means it starts high and gently falls like a hill. The authors found that you must match the length of that hill to the number of tokens you plan to train on. If you set the schedule for one hundred and thirty billion tokens but stop early, your early loss looks worse than it should, and this mistake is part of why Kaplan over-favored big models. In all of the good runs, the learning rate was decayed by ten times over roughly the actual number of training tokens.

After all three methods predicted that a Gopher-sized budget should use a model between forty and seventy billion parameters, the authors trained Chinchilla at seventy billion parameters for one point four trillion tokens. Chinchilla used the same MassiveText dataset as Gopher but with a slightly different mix of web pages, books, news, code, and Wikipedia. It used eighty layers with a model width of eight thousand one hundred and ninety-two, sixty-four attention heads, a maximum learning rate of one times ten to the minus four, and a batch size that doubled from one point five million to three million tokens halfway through. It was trained with the AdamW optimizer instead of Adam, in bfloat16 precision with a float32 copy of the weights, on TPUv3 and TPUv4 chips with JAX and Haiku software.

## Key results and numbers from the paper (with the actual scores/tables described in words)

The headline result is that Chinchilla beats much bigger models while using the same training FLOPs. On the Massive Multitask Language Understanding benchmark, called MMLU, which has fifty-seven exam-like subjects, Chinchilla got sixty-seven point six percent average accuracy with five examples in the prompt. Gopher got sixty point zero percent, GPT-3 got forty-three point nine percent, average human raters got thirty-four point five percent, and random guessing would get twenty-five percent. This was even higher than expert forecasters had predicted for mid-2023. Chinchilla beat Gopher on fifty-one of the fifty-seven MMLU subjects, tied on two, and lost on only four, which were college mathematics, econometrics, moral scenarios, and formal logic. It scored over ninety percent on high school government and politics, international law, sociology, and United States foreign policy.

On the Pile language-modeling collection, Chinchilla had lower bits-per-byte than Gopher on every single subset, which means it predicted text more efficiently everywhere. Compared to the one-hundred-and-seventy-eight-billion-parameter Jurassic-1, Chinchilla was better on all subsets except two, which were the mathematics set and the Ubuntu chat set. On Wikitext103, Chinchilla reached a perplexity of seven point one six, while Gopher reached seven point seven five, where lower perplexity means less surprise.

On reading comprehension, Chinchilla got seventy-seven point four percent on LAMBADA, ahead of Gopher at seventy-four point five percent and Megatron-Turing NLG at seventy-six point six percent. On RACE-middle it got eighty-six point eight percent versus seventy-five point one percent for Gopher, and on RACE-hard it got eighty-two point three percent versus seventy-one point six percent for Gopher, which are gains of more than ten points.

On the BIG-bench collection of sixty-two hard tasks, Chinchilla averaged sixty-five point one percent versus fifty-four point four percent for Gopher, which is a ten point seven percent improvement. It beat Gopher on all but four tasks.

On common sense tests, Chinchilla got eighty point eight percent on HellaSwag, eighty-one point eight percent on PIQA, seventy-four point nine percent on Winogrande, fifty-one point three percent on SIQA, and eighty-three point seven percent on BoolQ, matching or beating Gopher, GPT-3, and usually even the five-hundred-and-thirty-billion-parameter Megatron-Turing model. On TruthfulQA it jumped from twenty-nine point five percent zero-shot for Gopher to forty-three point six percent for Chinchilla.

On closed-book question answering, Chinchilla set new records on Natural Questions with thirty-one point five percent at five-shot and thirty-five point five percent at sixty-four-shot, compared to twenty-four point five percent and twenty-eight point two percent for Gopher. On TriviaQA unfiltered it got seventy-three point two percent at five-shot versus sixty-three point six percent for Gopher, beating GPT-3 as well.

The paper also showed that Chinchilla is cheaper to use. Because it is four times smaller than Gopher, it needs much less memory and much less computer power for every answer it gives after training. The authors stress that training cost happens once, but inference cost happens every time someone uses the model, so a smaller and smarter model saves energy for its whole lifetime.

## Why this paper matters (what later work it unlocked — name the specific later papers among the 15 where relevant)

This paper changed how the whole field trains language models. After Chinchilla, almost no serious team trained a giant model on only three hundred billion tokens again. The LLaMA-2 paper, which is number thirteen in our set of fifteen landmark papers, explicitly cites Chinchilla and trains a seventy-billion-parameter LLaMA-2 model on two trillion tokens, which is very close to the Chinchilla prescription, and its smaller seven-billion and thirteen-billion models are also trained much longer than older models. The DeepSeek-R1 paper, which is number fifteen, builds on that data-rich philosophy by training with massive high-quality data and long training runs rather than just adding parameters. The InstructGPT paper, which is number eight, and the DPO paper, which is number fourteen, both benefit because they start from base models that are now Chinchilla-optimal and therefore cheaper to fine-tune and to run during human-feedback training. In short, Chinchilla taught the field that careful data scaling beats blind parameter scaling, and every efficient open model since then follows that lesson.

## Honest limitations

The authors are honest that they only have two truly large runs to compare, which are Gopher and Chinchilla. All of the other four hundred runs are much smaller, so they must extrapolate far beyond what they directly measured when they predict what a trillion-parameter model should do. They also assume that the best trade-off follows a clean power law, but they show in an appendix figure that there is some curvature at high computer budgets, which suggests that even smaller models than they predict might be best. All of their scaling runs use less than one full pass over the dataset, so they do not study what happens when you repeat the same data for many epochs. Finally, training on one point four trillion tokens raises worries about data quality, test-set leakage, toxicity, bias, and privacy, because a bigger scrape of the web contains more of everything, both good and bad. The paper reports that Chinchilla still shows gender bias on the Winogender test and generates toxic text at about the same low rate as Gopher, so better training does not automatically fix safety problems.

## Fun trivia and history (from your web search)

The name Chinchilla is a biology joke. DeepMind had previously built a family of models named Gopher, after a chunky burrowing rodent. When they built a smaller animal to beat the big gopher, they named it Chinchilla, which is a much smaller and cuter rodent that still wins the race. The Wikipedia page for Chinchilla explains that both families were trained to study scaling laws, and the smaller animal winning was exactly the point.

The paper won an Outstanding Paper award at NeurIPS 2022, which is one of the highest honors in machine learning. Soon after it appeared, a rival model called PaLM with five hundred and forty billion parameters and seven hundred and sixty-eight billion tokens beat Chinchilla on some tasks despite not being compute-optimal. The DeepMind team responded in a blog post that this was expected, because PaLM used about five times more computer power overall, and they predicted that a one-hundred-and-forty-billion-parameter model on three trillion tokens would have been even better for that budget.

A famous quote from the work is that current large language models are significantly under-trained, a consequence of keeping training data constant while growing model size. That single sentence ended the era of three-hundred-billion-token training and started the era of trillion-token training that gave us LLaMA, Mistral, and many others.

## Key terms explained (glossary of 5-8 terms)

FLOPs means floating-point operations, which is a count of how many basic math operations training performs. It is the currency you spend to train, and the paper treats total FLOPs as fixed.

Parameters, written as N, are the adjustable knobs inside the model. More knobs mean the model can remember more patterns, but each knob costs computer power to train and to use.

Tokens, written as D, are the pieces of words the model reads during training. One point four trillion tokens is roughly one trillion words of books, web pages, code, and news.

Training loss measures how surprised the model is by real text. Lower loss means the model predicts the next word more accurately.

MMLU stands for Massive Multitask Language Understanding. It is a test with fifty-seven school and professional subjects, from astronomy to law, that checks broad knowledge.

IsoFLOP means equal computer budget. An IsoFLOP curve compares many different model sizes that all cost the same total FLOPs, so you can see which size wins.

Power law means a relationship that looks like a straight line when you plot both axes on logarithmic scales. It lets you predict far beyond your measurements with a simple exponent.

Inference cost means the computer power needed to actually use a trained model to answer questions. Smaller models are much cheaper at inference, which matters because a popular model may answer billions of questions.

## Connections (which earlier paper it builds on, which later paper builds on it)

This paper builds directly on the GPT-3 few-shot paper, which is number three in our set, because GPT-3 set the pattern of one-hundred-and-seventy-five billion parameters on three hundred billion tokens that Chinchilla proves was wasteful. It also builds on the Attention Is All You Need paper, which is number one, because all of the models it studies are Transformers, and on the BERT paper, which is number two, because BERT showed the power of large-scale pre-training that scaling laws try to optimize. It corrects the earlier Kaplan scaling-law work that is cited throughout the paper.

Later, the LLaMA-2 paper, which is number thirteen, builds on Chinchilla by adopting its token-rich training recipe. The InstructGPT paper, which is number eight, the chain-of-thought paper, which is number nine, the DPO paper, which is number fourteen, and the DeepSeek-R1 paper, which is number fifteen, all benefit from starting with Chinchilla-optimal base models that are smaller, cheaper, and smarter, which makes alignment, reasoning, and inference much more practical.
