# LLaMA 2 — Open Foundation and Fine-Tuned Chat Models
**Authors:** Hugo Touvron, Louis Martin, Kevin Stone, Peter Albert, Amjad Almahairi, Yasmine Babaei, Nikolay Bashlykov, Soumya Batra, Prajjwal Bhargava, Shruti Bhosale, Dan Bikel, Lukas Blecher, Cristian Canton Ferrer, Moya Chen, Guillem Cucurull, David Esiobu, Jude Fernandes, Jeremy Fu, Wenyin Fu, Brian Fuller, Cynthia Gao, Vedanuj Goswami, Naman Goyal, Anthony Hartshorn, Saghar Hosseini, Rui Hou, Hakan Inan, Marcin Kardas, Viktor Kerkez, Madian Khabsa, Isabel Kloumann, Artem Korenev, Punit Singh Koura, Marie-Anne Lachaux, Thibaut Lavril, Jenya Lee, Diana Liskovich, Yinghai Lu, Yuning Mao, Xavier Martinet, Todor Mihaylov, Pushkar Mishra, Igor Molybog, Yixin Nie, Andrew Poulton, Jeremy Reizenstein, Rashi Rungta, Kalyan Saladi, Alan Schelten, Ruan Silva, Eric Michael Smith, Ranjan Subramanian, Xiaoqing Ellen Tan, Binh Tang, Ross Taylor, Adina Williams, Jian Xiang Kuan, Puxin Xu, Zheng Yan, Iliyan Zarov, Yuchen Zhang, Angela Fan, Melanie Kambadur, Sharan Narang, Aurelien Rodriguez, Robert Stojnic, Sergey Edunov, Thomas Scialom and many collaborators — GenAI, Meta

## The story in one paragraph
LLaMA 2 is Meta's family of large language models that was released openly with both raw pretrained versions and polished chat versions. The paper describes models from 7 billion to 70 billion parameters that were trained on two trillion tokens of public text, then carefully taught to be helpful dialogue assistants through supervised instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback, with a very large emphasis on safety testing and red teaming. The biggest message is that an openly released model, when it is tuned with enough human care, can come close to closed commercial products like ChatGPT on human judgments of helpfulness and safety, and that describing the whole recipe in public helps the entire research community build safer models together.

## What problem was hurting before this paper
Before LLaMA 2, there was a painful split in the world of language models. On one side, there were powerful closed product assistants like ChatGPT, Bard, and Claude. These assistants felt useful and relatively safe because companies had spent a huge amount of money on human annotation, instruction tuning, and reinforcement learning from human feedback, but nobody outside the company could see how they were built or reproduce them. On the other side, there were open pretrained models like BLOOM, Falcon, MPT, and the first LLaMA 1. These open models were good at predicting text and matched closed base models like GPT-3 and Chinchilla on academic tests, but they were not good chatbots out of the box. They had not received the expensive alignment step, so they were hard to use safely as assistants. Researchers who wanted to study alignment were therefore stuck, because the most important part of the process was hidden behind closed doors and was too costly and unclear to copy. LLaMA 2 was written to fix that gap by giving away both strong base models and strong chat models, along with a detailed explanation of how the chat tuning and safety work were actually done.

## The core idea, explained simply (with an analogy)
The core idea is that openness plus careful human teaching can produce a free assistant that is almost as good as an expensive private one. Think of a base pretrained model like a very smart student who has read an entire library but has never learned manners for a dinner party. That student knows many facts but will interrupt, ramble, or say rude things without realizing it. LLaMA 2 first builds a stronger student by letting it read much more and remember longer conversations than LLaMA 1 did. Then it sends that student to a long finishing school where human teachers show good examples, rank better and worse answers, and repeatedly correct unsafe behavior until the student learns to be helpful, honest, and careful. An everyday analogy is a community recipe book. Instead of keeping the secret sauce private like a restaurant chain, Meta published the ingredients, the cooking steps, the taste tests, and the safety checks, so that any small bakery could bake a similar cake without having to redo years of expensive experiments from scratch.

## How it actually works (step by step: architecture upgrades over LLaMA 1, pretraining data and scale, fine-tuning SFT + RLHF, ghost attention, safety tuning — explain each piece in words)

**Step 1: Architecture upgrades over LLaMA 1, explained gently.**
LLaMA 2 keeps the same general Transformer skeleton as LLaMA 1, which means it still uses pre-normalization with RMSNorm, the SwiGLU activation function that helps information flow smoothly, and rotary positional embeddings that tell the model about word order. The paper makes two main changes. First, the context window is doubled from 2048 tokens to 4096 tokens, which means the model can keep about twice as much recent conversation in mind at once. This is especially helpful for chat, summarization, and long documents, and ablations show it helps on long-context tests like SCROLLS without hurting normal tasks. Second, the larger 34B and 70B models switch to grouped-query attention, usually called GQA. In normal multi-head attention, every attention head keeps its own full copy of keys and values for past tokens, which makes the memory cache very large during generation. GQA lets groups of heads share the same keys and values, so the memory cache becomes much smaller and inference becomes faster with large batches. The paper tests this carefully and finds that GQA with eight key-value projections performs almost as well as full attention and clearly better than sharing everything with only one projection. The tokenizer is unchanged from LLaMA 1, with a vocabulary of 32 thousand pieces learned with byte-pair encoding.

**Step 2: Pretraining data and scale, explained in plain words.**
The team trained on a new mix of publicly available online data, with no data from Meta products, and they tried to remove websites known to contain a lot of private personal information. They trained on two trillion tokens, which is about 40 percent more than LLaMA 1, because they found that models were still improving and had not saturated even at that size. They also up-sampled the most factual sources on purpose, which means they showed the model reliable texts more often, hoping to increase knowledge and reduce hallucinations. Training used a global batch size of four million tokens, the AdamW optimizer, a cosine learning rate schedule with warmup, weight decay, and gradient clipping. Training ran on Meta's Research Super Cluster and internal production clusters with NVIDIA A100 graphics cards and two different networks, InfiniBand and the cheaper RoCE Ethernet, and the paper notes that the cheaper network scaled almost as well up to two thousand GPUs. In total this took about 3.3 million GPU hours and emitted an estimated 539 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent, which Meta says was fully offset, and releasing the models openly means others do not need to repeat that expensive training.

**Step 3: Supervised fine-tuning, where quality beats quantity.**
Supervised fine-tuning, often shortened to SFT, is the first teaching stage where humans write good prompts and ideal answers and the model learns to imitate them. The team started with public instruction data, but found that much of it lacked diversity and quality for dialogue, so they collected their own high-quality vendor annotations. A striking lesson was that setting aside millions of lower-quality third-party examples and using only tens of thousands of carefully written examples worked much better. They stopped after 27,540 SFT annotations. They concatenated prompts and answers to fill 4096-token sequences, used a special separator token, computed loss only on answer tokens so the model was not punished for the user's words, and fine-tuned for two epochs with a small learning rate. They noticed that the resulting SFT model often wrote answers as good as human annotators, which told them to spend most future annotation effort on preference comparisons for the next stage instead of more SFT writing.

**Step 4: Reinforcement learning from human feedback, the main polishing loop.**
Reinforcement learning from human feedback, usually called RLHF, is the longer stage where humans do not write perfect answers but instead compare two model answers and pick the better one, and the model learns a sense of taste from those choices. The team collected over 1.4 million binary human comparisons in 14 weekly batches, which together with open-source preference data made about 2.9 million comparisons. They asked annotators to judge helpfulness and safety separately, and to say how strong the preference was, from slightly better to significantly better. From this data they trained two separate reward models, one for helpfulness and one for safety, because one model trying to do both got confused when being very helpful conflicted with refusing an unsafe request. Both reward models were started from chat checkpoints so they already knew what the chat model knew, and they were trained with a ranking loss plus a margin that forces more clearly different answers to get more clearly different scores. Then they improved the chat model iteratively through versions called RLHF-V1 through RLHF-V5 using two algorithms. Rejection sampling means sampling many answers, often tens of answers at higher temperature, scoring them with the reward model, and fine-tuning on the best one, which is broad exploration. PPO, which stands for Proximal Policy Optimization, means gently nudging the model's policy to get higher reward while staying close to the original model with a KL penalty to avoid reward hacking and instability. They only ran rejection sampling with the largest 70B model and then distilled its best answers down into the 7B and 13B models, and they found they needed to re-tune the sampling temperature as training progressed and to keep old good samples to avoid forgetting skills like rhyming.

**Step 5: Ghost Attention, a clever trick for remembering instructions.**
Ghost Attention, shortened to GAtt, solves a very relatable chat problem where the model forgets a standing instruction after a few turns, for example to always answer briefly or to always speak as a certain persona. The trick is inspired by context distillation and works by hacking the training data. The team takes a normal multi-turn dialogue, synthetically pastes the same instruction onto every user turn, generates a good consistent answer with the latest model, and then for fine-tuning they keep the instruction only in the first turn but set the loss to zero on all earlier turns so the model must learn to pay attention back to that first message. They created synthetic constraints like hobbies, languages, and public figures, sometimes combined and shortened, and fine-tuned on that data after RLHF-V3. Human tests showed the model with GAtt stayed consistent 100 percent of the time even after 20 turns, while the model without it dropped to near zero after only a few turns, and attention visualizations show the model keeps looking back at the system message much longer.

**Step 6: Safety tuning woven through every stage.**
Safety is treated as a long-tail problem where rare tricky prompts cause most harm, so the team adds safety at three levels. First, supervised safety fine-tuning includes adversarial prompts that annotators deliberately designed to trick the model, plus safe demonstrations that first address immediate danger, explain the risk, and then give helpful lawful alternatives. Second, safety RLHF adds a dedicated safety reward model, many more adversarial prompts during sampling and PPO, and a piecewise rule that uses the safety score when a prompt looks risky or the safety score is very low. Experiments show that adding more safety data, up to about one hundred thousand safety samples alongside nine hundred thousand helpfulness samples, shifts safety scores strongly upward and thins out the dangerous tail while helpfulness scores stay flat, though borderline prompts with sensitive words like bomb or crack get refused more often. Third, safety context distillation prefixes an adversarial prompt with a safety preprompt such as you are a safe and responsible assistant, or even a risk-specific template that tells the model how to structure a refusal, generates a safer answer, and then fine-tunes the model to produce that safer answer without needing the preprompt, but only when the reward model confirms the new answer is actually better, because otherwise the model can become vague or refuse harmless questions.

## Key results and numbers from the paper (benchmark scores described in words, human eval win rates)
On academic tests of the raw pretrained models, LLaMA 2 is clearly stronger than LLaMA 1 and stronger than other open models like MPT and Falcon of similar size. The 70B base model is the headline result. It scores about 68.9 percent on MMLU, which is a large multi-subject knowledge test, about five points higher than LLaMA 1 with 65B parameters, and about 51.2 percent on Big Bench Hard, which is about eight points higher. On other grouped tests it reaches about 37.5 on code tasks, 71.9 on commonsense reasoning, 63.6 on world knowledge, 69.4 on reading comprehension, 35.2 on math, and 54.2 on AGI Eval English exams. Compared to closed base models, LLaMA 2 70B is close to GPT-3.5 on MMLU with 68.9 versus 70.0 and close on grade-school math GSM8K with 56.8 versus 57.1, and it matches or beats the very large PaLM with 540B parameters on almost all tests, but it still trails GPT-4 and PaLM-2-L by a large margin, especially on coding where HumanEval is only 29.9 versus 48.1 for GPT-3.5 and 67.0 for GPT-4.

For the chat models, the paper leans heavily on model-based and human judgments rather than only academic scores. After several RLHF rounds, LLaMA 2-Chat beats ChatGPT on the team's own reward models for both helpfulness and harmlessness, and when GPT-4 is used as a more neutral judge it still wins more than 60 percent of non-tied comparisons. Human helpfulness evaluation on about four thousand single-turn and multi-turn prompts with three raters per prompt found that LLaMA 2-Chat 7B beats MPT-7B-chat on 60 percent of prompts, and LLaMA 2-Chat 34B wins more than 75 percent overall against Vicuna-33B and Falcon 40B of similar size. The largest 70B chat model is competitive with ChatGPT with a 36 percent win rate and a 31.5 percent tie rate, and it beats PaLM's chat model by a large margin, though the authors warn that prompts did not include coding or reasoning, only final turns were judged, and human judgments are noisy and subjective. On safety, human evaluation on about two thousand adversarial prompts found low violation rates that are comparable to or better than ChatGPT and Falcon and clearly better than MPT and Vicuna, with multi-turn attacks harder than single-turn ones, and automatic tests show big gains from tuning, with truthfulness rising from about 50.2 to 64.1 percent for the 70B model and toxic generations on ToxiGen falling from about 24.6 percent to effectively zero percent.

## Why this paper matters (the open-weights ecosystem it unlocked)
This paper matters less because of a single new equation and more because of what it gave away and explained. By releasing 7B, 13B, and 70B base and chat weights for research and commercial use with starter code, plus a 34B result reported but held back for more red teaming, Meta gave startups, researchers, and hobbyists a free foundation that was close to commercial quality for the first time. That unlocked an explosion of fine-tunes, quantized versions that run on smaller GPUs and even laptops, domain-specific assistants, evaluation harnesses, and safety research that did not need to pay for pretraining or API bills. The unusually detailed description of SFT data curation, dual reward models, iterative rejection sampling plus PPO, Ghost Attention, safety distillation, and red teaming also taught the community how alignment is actually done in practice, including lessons like quality beats quantity for SFT, reward models need fresh on-distribution data every iteration, and safety data can fix the worst failures without destroying helpfulness. In short, LLaMA 2 turned alignment from a closed secret into a shared engineering recipe and helped shift the industry debate from only API access toward downloadable weights that can be inspected, modified, and deployed privately.

## Honest limitations
The authors are unusually candid that LLaMA 2-Chat still has familiar flaws. It can hallucinate facts, give unqualified medical, legal, or financial advice without proper disclaimers, and its knowledge stops after pretraining so it does not know recent events. It is English-centric because almost 90 percent of pretraining documents are English, so other languages are fragile and safety is much less tested outside English. The base models are deliberately left unfiltered for toxicity so they can be useful for tasks like hate-speech detection, which means raw LLaMA 2 must never be deployed as a chatbot without additional safety tuning. Safety tuning can also go too far, making the model overly cautious with false refusals on harmless borderline prompts that happen to contain words like bomb in bomb drink or crack in Christmas crack dessert. Human and automatic evaluations are limited by small prompt sets, subjective guidelines, missing coding and reasoning coverage, and content standards that may favor LLaMA 2-Chat itself, and red teaming so far has been English-focused. Finally, open release itself is dual-use, because the same weights that let good actors build helpful tools can be misused for misinformation, cybercrime, or weapons advice despite tuning to refuse those topics.

## Fun trivia and history (from your web search)
One of the most famous stories is the original LLaMA leak that set the stage for LLaMA 2. In February 2023 Meta offered the first LLaMA weights only to approved researchers under a non-commercial license, but within about a week a torrent link appeared on the anonymous forum 4chan and quickly spread to GitHub, Hugging Face, torrents, and Discord. Researchers confirmed the leak was real, and Meta began filing takedowns, but it was too late. People ran the 13B version on a MacBook Pro and built chatbots from it, and Stanford's Alpaca showed you could instruction-tune it cheaply, which proved enormous demand for open weights and pushed Meta to make LLaMA 2 properly open for commercial use with Microsoft, AWS, and Hugging Face as launch partners. A second fun debate is about the word open itself. LLaMA 2 was celebrated as open source and got more than 150,000 download requests in its first week, with Yann LeCun calling it a landscape-changing moment and The Verge calling it the biggest AI release since ChatGPT, but the Open Source Initiative and many commentators objected that it is not truly open source because the custom Community License requires giants with over 700 million monthly users to ask Meta for special permission and forbids using outputs to improve other large models. That argument created the now-common distinction between open weights or source-available versus strict OSI-approved open source.

## Key terms explained (glossary of 5-8 terms)
**Pretrained base model:** A model that has only learned to predict the next word from huge amounts of text. It knows a lot but does not yet know how to behave as a polite assistant, so it may continue a question with more questions instead of answering helpfully.

**Fine-tuned chat model:** A base model that has received extra teaching on dialogue examples and human preferences so it learns to follow instructions, stay in character across turns, refuse dangerous requests kindly, and give structured helpful answers.

**Supervised fine-tuning (SFT):** Learning by imitating good examples that humans wrote. The model sees many ideal prompt-and-answer pairs and adjusts itself to write more like those demonstrations.

**Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF):** Learning by developing taste from comparisons. Humans say which of two answers they prefer, a reward model learns to predict that preference as a number, and the chat model is then optimized to earn higher numbers.

**Reward model:** A separate scoring model that reads a prompt and an answer and outputs a single quality score. LLaMA 2 uses two of them, one that scores helpfulness and one that scores safety, because trying to balance both in one score is confusing.

**Grouped-query attention (GQA):** A memory-saving version of attention where several attention heads share the same keys and values for past tokens instead of each keeping a full private copy. This makes the cache smaller and generation faster, especially for large models and long contexts.

**Ghost Attention (GAtt):** Meta's trick for helping a chatbot remember a system instruction like speak briefly or act as a certain persona across many turns. It trains on synthetic dialogues where the instruction is pasted everywhere to create good examples, then teaches the model to produce the same consistent behavior when the instruction appears only once at the start.

**Red teaming:** Deliberate attack testing where hundreds of people, including security, law, misinformation, and civil-rights experts, try to trick the model into saying something unsafe. The failures are then fed back into training, much like crash tests that make future cars safer.

## Connections (which earlier papers it builds on: 01 Transformer, 03 GPT-3, 08 InstructGPT, 10 Chinchilla, 12 FlashAttention; which later papers build on it)
LLaMA 2 stands directly on the shoulders of the Transformer, because its entire architecture is an optimized auto-regressive Transformer with RMSNorm, SwiGLU, and rotary embeddings for positions. It follows GPT-3 in showing that a large pretrained Transformer can be a few-shot learner across knowledge, commonsense, and code, and it copies the same style of broad academic evaluation. It copies and extends InstructGPT by using supervised instruction tuning plus reward modeling plus PPO to align a base model to human preferences, but it adds the innovations of dual helpfulness and safety reward models, large-scale iterative rejection sampling distilled from 70B to small models, and Ghost Attention for multi-turn system messages. It absorbs the lesson of Chinchilla that the amount of training tokens matters as much as parameter count, which is why it trains on two trillion tokens and up-samples factual data to reduce hallucinations. While it does not claim a new FlashAttention kernel, its use of grouped-query attention and its careful engineering for fast inference with tensor parallelism, KV caching, and FSDP training address the same memory and speed concerns that motivate efficient attention work. Later, LLaMA 2 became a foundation for a huge family of descendants, including Vicuna-style chat fine-tunes, Code Llama for programming, Llama Guard for safety classification, medical, legal, and multilingual adaptations, quantization and LoRA fine-tuning methods that made it runnable on consumer hardware, and eventually Llama 3, while its release strategy also shaped the policy debate about open weights, responsible use guides, and what counts as truly open source.
