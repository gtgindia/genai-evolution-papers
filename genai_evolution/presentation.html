2017 · Attention Is All You Need · Vaswani et al., Google Brain · NIPS 2017 Read Paper 01 notes

Attention Is All You Need — throw away the loop

1 · PROBLEM — THE FINGER READER

Machine translation (MT) systems built from long short-term memory (LSTM) encoder–decoders had to process one word at a time, using the recurrence ht=f(ht−1,xt). As a result, word ten had to wait for word nine, words in one example could not be processed in parallel, and long sentences were squeezed through a single summary that could lose early details. Bahdanau attention let a decoder look back at source words, but it was still attached to a recurrent neural network (RNN). Convolutional neural network (CNN) alternatives such as ByteNet and ConvS2S allowed parallel processing, yet required O(log n)–O(n) layers for distant words to interact; long-range relationships such as verb agreement remained difficult, and training still took weeks.

2 · HOW IT WORKS — 3 STEPS

  1. Architecture: The encoder has six layers, each combining self-attention with a feed-forward network (FFN) that maps 512 dimensions to 2048 and back to 512. The decoder also has six layers and combines masked self-attention, encoder–decoder cross-attention, and an FFN. Residual connections and layer normalization wrap the sublayers. The model dimension is d=512, it uses h=8 attention heads of width dk=64, and sinusoidal positional encodings use sin(pos/100002i/d). There is no recurrence.
  2. Training: The team trained on 4.5 million sentence pairs from the 2014 Workshop on Machine Translation (WMT14) English–German task and 36 million English–French pairs, using WordPiece vocabularies of 37,000 and 32,000 tokens. They used the Adam optimizer with β=(0.9,0.98), a 4,000-step learning-rate warmup followed by inverse-square-root decay, dropout of 0.1, and label smoothing of 0.1. Attention computes softmax(QKT/√d)V.
  3. Decoding: The decoder generates one output token at a time while a causal mask prevents it from seeing future tokens. It uses beam search with a beam width of four and a length penalty of 0.6, and predictions are averaged from the final 5–20 training checkpoints.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

Self-attention gives any pair of words a direct connection, so their maximum interaction path is O(1) rather than O(n); a reference can remain available instead of being blurred through a long recurrent chain. The model expresses these comparisons as matrix multiplications, allowing graphics processing units (GPUs) to compute many of the n² token-to-token scores in parallel. Its eight heads can learn different relationships, such as pronoun reference, tense, or word alignment, while the FFN processes each position’s representation. The design therefore trades sequential memory access for parallel computation.

score = Q·K / √d → softmax → ×V
In plain terms, each word compares itself with every other word, scales the scores so they remain stable, converts them into attention weights, and uses those weights to combine information.
◍ The title alludes to the Beatles’ “All You Need Is Love,” and the paper says the equal-contribution authors are listed in random order. All eight authors later left Google; at a 2024 Nvidia GTC reunion, seven joined Jensen Huang onstage and joked that the world needed something better than Transformers.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE THE PAPER: LOOPS, WHISPERS, AND SLOW TRANSLATION

  • Step 1 — What the game is: This area is sequence transduction — turning one sequence into another, like translating an English sentence into German or turning words into a grammar tree. You should care because every translator, voice assistant, and chatbot you touch is some form of this machinery. In 2017 the flagship was machine translation, scored by BLEU: an automatic metric that compares machine output to human reference translations by matching word sequences, where higher means closer to human.
  • Step 2 — The words you need: The standard design was the encoder-decoder architecture: an encoder reads the whole input sentence into memory vectors, and a decoder writes the output one word at a time while consulting that memory. Generation is autoregressive — each new word may only depend on words already produced. The network’s living summary of everything seen so far is its hidden state, a vector carried along the sentence — and that carried vector is exactly what this paper attacks.
  • Step 3 — How it worked before: The best systems were recurrent neural networks — long short-term memory and gated recurrent units — stacked into encoder-decoders. They generated hidden states step by step along the sentence: computation for word ten cannot start until word nine finishes, and information traveled like a whisper down a row. Attention already existed as a bridge letting the decoder look back at relevant source words regardless of distance, but it was bolted onto recurrence. Convolutional rivals like ByteNet and ConvS2S ran in parallel, yet linked distant words only through many stacked layers.
  • Step 4 — Where it broke: The sequential bottleneck made parallelization almost impossible inside one example, and memory limits blocked the usual fix of huge batches, especially for long sentences. Long-range links degraded like a whispered message: match a verb with a phrase twenty words away and the signal arrived blurry, because the information path was long and lossy. Training the best systems cost enormous compute while quality plateaued — and bolted-on attention never removed the bottleneck itself.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — THE IDEA, BUILT PIECE BY PIECE: ATTENTION WITHOUT THE LOOP

  • Step 5 — Throw away the loop: Rely only on attention: every position talks to every other position in one step — each word asks a question, compares it to labels on all words, and takes a weighted average of their meanings. The paper’s classroom analogy: the old room whispered messages down the row, so the last student heard a blurry version of the first; the Transformer room lets every student turn around, see everyone, and listen most to the relevant voices. This is self-attention — queries, keys, and values all come from the same sentence.
  • Step 6 — The two stacks: The encoder is six identical layers, each with multi-head self-attention then a position-wise feed-forward network (width 512, inner layer 2048, ReLU), every part wrapped in a residual connection plus layer normalization — add the input back to keep signals flowing, then normalize to keep training stable. The decoder is six layers of three parts: masked self-attention over outputs already generated, attention over the encoder memory (classic translation alignment), and the same feed-forward. Masking sets illegal future connections to negative infinity before the softmax, so position five cannot peek at six and beyond.
  • Step 7 — The famous formula: Scaled dot-product attention has three ingredients: a query is the current word’s question, a key a label on another word, a value the content that word offers. Take the dot product of query with every key (multiply matching numbers and add), divide by √d so scores cannot explode as vectors lengthen, softmax into weights summing to one, then take the weighted sum of values. Why scale? Unnormalized dot products grow with dimension, pushing softmax into flat, gradient-starved regions; √d restores variance one. Multi-head attention runs this eight times on 64-dimensional projections — one head tracks verb phrases, another links its → law.
  • Step 8 — Order, training, generation: With no recurrence or convolution the model would have no sense of word order, so sine and cosine positional encodings of many frequencies are added to the embeddings — for any fixed offset, shifted positions become a linear function of the original, hinting at relative positions. Training asks the model to predict each next target word, with label smoothing 0.1 making it less overconfident; Adam with 4000 warmup steps then inverse-square-root decay optimizes it. At inference the decoder generates autoregressively with beam search size 4 and length penalty 0.6.
  • Step 9 — Walk one sentence through: Take a clause the paper visualizes, with the pronoun its referring back to law several words earlier. In the recurrent whisper chain that link crosses many lossy hops; in self-attention, when its builds a query, the key on law scores highly, softmax turns that into a large weight, and the value of law flows straight into its new representation — one hop, not twenty. Meanwhile encoder-decoder attention aligns each output word with its source words, and positional encodings keep order straight. All positions do this at once, as matrix math.

STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — THE PROOF, THE LIMITS, AND WHAT IT UNLOCKED

  • Step 10 — What the numbers proved: On WMT 2014 English→German the big model reached 28.4 BLEU, beating the best previous systems including ensembles by more than two points — and even the base model’s 27.3 topped every published result. On English→French the big model hit 41.8 BLEU at less than a quarter of the training cost of the previous best. The base model trained in about 12 hours on eight P100 GPUs, roughly 0.4 seconds per step, while the old champions spent vastly more compute for worse scores.
  • Step 11 — Why it works: Any two words are connected in one step, so long-range agreement — a verb matching a faraway phrase — gets a direct gradient path instead of a long chain of hops, and every position computes in parallel with optimized matrix multiplication rather than a serial loop. Multi-head attention offers several simultaneous listening strategies in subspaces at near single-head cost. The ablations agree: eight heads beat one by about 0.9 BLEU and larger dimensions help, while 32 heads hurt — capacity must be divided sensibly.
  • Step 12 — Honest limits: Self-attention costs quadratically in sequence length, so long documents, high-resolution images, audio, and video stay expensive — the authors suggest restricted local attention as future work. Averaging attention-weighted positions reduces effective resolution, which multi-head only partly fixes. The sinusoidal positional encoding is a hypothesis, not a proven extrapolation solution. Label smoothing improves BLEU while worsening perplexity — useful overconfidence control, not fully understood. Parsing still trails the best task-specific grammars, and evaluation covered only two language pairs with BLEU.
  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: Nearly the whole deck. BERT (2) takes the encoder stack for bidirectional understanding; GPT-3 (3) scales the masked decoder to 175B parameters; RAG (4) wraps Transformer generators with retrievers; DDPM (5) and Latent Diffusion (7) build attention-based image models — the authors explicitly predicted images, audio, and video. CLIP (6), InstructGPT (8), Chain-of-Thought (9), Chinchilla (10), LoRA (11), FlashAttention (12), LLaMA 2 (13), DPO (14), and DeepSeek-R1 (15) all assume this backbone, then add contrastive learning, human feedback, prompting, scaling laws, efficiency, or reasoning on top.
2018 · BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding · Devlin et al., Google AI · NAACL Best Long Read Paper 02 notes

BERT — understand by filling blanks both ways

1 · PROBLEM — HALF-BLIND READERS

ELMo combined a left-to-right and a right-to-left long short-term memory (LSTM) network, but each direction was trained separately, so its representations were never deeply bidirectional. The original Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) used a Transformer with a causal mask: it could see earlier words but not later ones, so a representation of bank could not use a following word such as river to resolve its meaning. Question answering (QA) over text spans and natural language inference (NLI) both benefit from using context on both sides. Task-specific networks still lagged, and although pretraining helped, each task generally required a different architecture.

2 · HOW — 3 STEPS

  1. Architecture: BERT, short for Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers, is an encoder-only Transformer. The Base model has 12 layers, width 768, 12 attention heads, and 110 million parameters; the Large model has 24 layers, width 1,024, 16 heads, and 340 million parameters. Both use GELU activations, a 30,000-piece WordPiece vocabulary, and special CLS and SEP tokens, with token, segment, and position embeddings.
  2. Pretraining: The model was trained on 800 million words from BooksCorpus and 2.5 billion words from Wikipedia. For masked-language modeling, 15% of tokens are selected: 80% are replaced by MASK, 10% by a random token, and 10% are kept unchanged; loss is calculated only on selected positions. A second objective, next-sentence prediction (NSP), uses the CLS representation to distinguish a true following sentence from a random one in a 50/50 mix. Training runs for one million steps with batches of 256 sequences, or 128,000 tokens, and Adam at 1e-4.
  3. Fine-tuning: A small task-specific output layer is added, such as a CLS-times-W classifier for GLUE or start- and end-position vectors for SQuAD. All pretrained weights are then tuned for two to four epochs with a learning rate of 2–5e-5, which takes about one hour on a tensor processing unit (TPU).

3 · WHY IT WORKS

To fill in a hidden word, BERT must combine information from the words before and after it throughout every layer, while the corruption recipe avoids making the model depend only on a special MASK token at test time. The next-sentence objective adds a signal about whether two passages fit together, which ordinary next-word prediction does not directly provide. Together, filling gaps and checking sentence order teach reusable contextual representations that can be adapted to many tasks.

L = −log P(masked | both sides) − log P(IsNext | CLS)
In plain terms, BERT learns to predict hidden words from both neighbours and to judge whether the second sentence really follows the first.
◍ BERT is named after Bert from Sesame Street, continuing the playful Muppet naming tradition associated with ELMo. Google open-sourced the code and pretrained checkpoints, and deployed BERT in Search in October 2019 before expanding it to more than 70 languages by December 2019.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE THE PAPER: HALF-READ PAGES AND HAND-BUILT PIPELINES

  • Step 1 — What the game is: Language understanding — decide if a review is happy or sad, match a question to its answer passage, pick the most plausible continuation of a story. These tasks sit behind search, assistants, and spam filters. In 2018 progress was measured on benchmarks like GLUE (nine general understanding tasks), SQuAD (find the answer span in a Wikipedia passage), and SWAG (choose the plausible physical continuation). The dream was one model that could master all of them without hand-built plumbing per task.
  • Step 2 — The words you need: Two reuse recipes existed. The feature-based approach froze a pre-trained model, pulled out its word vectors as extra features, and trained a separate task-specific model on top. The fine-tuning approach initialized a task model with pre-trained weights and then trained all parameters end to end with just a small new output layer. Tokenization used WordPiece, a 30,000-piece subword vocabulary that splits rare words like playing into play + ing. Special symbols CLS and SEP mark sequence start and sentence boundaries, with CLS’s final vector serving as the aggregate representation for classification.
  • Step 3 — How it worked before: ELMo glued two one-directional language models — one left-to-right, one right-to-left — into context-sensitive word vectors: a shallow combination, not one deeply bidirectional network. OpenAI GPT fine-tuned a full Transformer, but constrained every token to attend only to previous tokens, because standard next-word prediction would cheat if it could see the future. Teams still engineered a different architecture per benchmark, and results lagged what deep bidirectional understanding should offer.
  • Step 4 — Where it broke: Left-to-right reading loses right-side context exactly where it matters. The glossary’s favorite example: the meaning of bank should use both river on the left and money on the right, but a one-way model must commit early. For question answering, pinpointing an answer span crucially needs right-side cues, so one-directional pre-training was very harmful on token-level tasks. And naively pre-training a deep bidirectional model is a trap: if every word sees both sides in many layers, it can indirectly see itself and trivially predict itself.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — THE IDEA, BUILT PIECE BY PIECE: LEARN BY FILLING BLANKS

  • Step 5 — Fill-in-the-blank, both ways: Train with cloze games instead of next-word prediction, so the model must use both sides to guess missing pieces — plus a coherence game: does sentence B truly follow sentence A? The exam analogy: the old student covered the right half of the page and guessed what came next; the BERT student takes tests where the teacher blanks words anywhere in the paragraph, reading left and right to fill gaps, then practices judging whether two paragraphs really connect. After months of these games, any new exam format takes only a few examples.
  • Step 6 — The architecture: A multi-layer bidirectional Transformer encoder — the encoder half of Attention Is All You Need (1) with the causal mask removed, so every token attends to every token at every layer. BERT base: 12 layers, hidden size 768, 12 attention heads, 110M parameters, sized to match OpenAI GPT for fair comparison; BERT large: 24 layers, hidden 1024, 16 heads, 340M. Input packing: CLS first, then tokens, with SEP between sentences; each token embedding is the sum of token, segment (sentence A or B), and position embeddings.
  • Step 7 — The first game, decoded: Masked language modeling hides 15% of WordPiece tokens at random and predicts only those positions, with cross-entropy loss over the vocabulary — in plain words the loss asks: given both sides of the blank, which exact word belonged here? The 80/10/10 rule patches a mismatch: of each chosen position, 80% become the mask token, 10% a random token, 10% stay unchanged — yet all are still predicted, so the model must keep good representations for every word, never knowing which will be queried.
  • Step 8 — The second game and fine-tuning: Next sentence prediction pairs real continuations (IsNext) with random sentences (NotNext) at 50/50, and the CLS vector does binary classification — the final model reaches 97–98% accuracy, and ablations show it strongly helps question answering and inference. Pre-training consumed 3.3 billion words of BooksCorpus plus Wikipedia, document-level so sequences stay contiguous. Fine-tuning then reuses everything with one new output layer: CLS multiplied by a classification matrix W through softmax, or for SQuAD, start and end vectors dot-producted against every token to score answer spans — most runs finish in about an hour on one Cloud TPU.
  • Step 9 — Walk one input through: Take the appendix’s tiny example: my dog is hairy. One run sees my dog is mask, another my dog is apple after a random swap, another leaves my dog is hairy intact — and every time the model must recover hairy. Left context (my dog is) is informative, right context is empty, and the network leans on whatever surrounds the blank; the corruption rule means the same surface sentence demands different defenses. Then at fine-tuning time: question packed as A embeddings, passage as B, and the same network scores where the answer starts and ends.

STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — THE PROOF, THE LIMITS, AND WHAT IT UNLOCKED

  • Step 10 — What the numbers proved: GLUE average hit 79.6 for base and 82.1 for large, versus 75.1 for OpenAI GPT and 74.0 for prior state of the art — the MNLI gain alone was 4.6 points. On SQuAD 1.1, BERT large reached 84.1 exact match and 90.9 F1 single-model, beating top leaderboard ensembles. SWAG reached 86.3 versus 78.0 for GPT. The ablation was the clincher: train left-to-right like GPT and SQuAD F1 falls from 88.5 to 77.8 — bidirectionality itself was worth ten points.
  • Step 11 — Why it works: Deep bidirectionality at every layer shapes each word by both sides — bank really does see river and money — while predicting only masked positions blocks trivial self-copy, so the model must learn genuine context-to-word mappings. The 80/10/10 corruption forces robust representations of every token, and next sentence prediction adds sentence-level coherence that pure language modeling misses. One pre-trained network plus a tiny head replaces an entire shelf of hand-built task architectures — and larger models help even on tiny datasets.
  • Step 12 — Honest limits: Predicting only 15% of tokens per batch converges slower than left-to-right modeling — about one million steps, four days on large TPU pods. Mask tokens never appear at fine-tuning time; 80/10/10 mitigates but does not remove that mismatch. Next sentence prediction is a coarse signal with random negatives, later found weaker than claimed. BERT large fine-tuning is unstable on small datasets, needing several restarts. The model is English-only, capped at 512 tokens, expensive to pre-train, and still needs labeled data per task.
  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: BERT ignited the pre-train then fine-tune era. GPT-3 (3) answers from the opposite direction, scaling a left-to-right decoder and prompting instead of fine-tuning. RAG (4) builds retrievers and generators on BERT-style encoders; CLIP (6) pairs BERT-like text Transformers with vision; InstructGPT (8) draws on BERT-style reward-model and evaluation ideas. Chinchilla (10) refines scaling lessons these ablations began, while LoRA (11), FlashAttention (12), LLaMA 2 (13), DPO (14), and DeepSeek-R1 (15) inherit the WordPiece, CLS/SEP, and fine-tuning playbook BERT standardized.

GPT-3 — don’t retrain, just prompt

1 · PROBLEM — LABEL POVERTY

The usual pretrain-then-fine-tune recipe required roughly 1,000–100,000 labelled examples for each task, making the long tail of tasks too expensive and encouraging brittle shortcuts that failed on out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs. People can often switch tasks after reading an instruction and one or two examples, without retraining. GPT-2 suggested that a model might learn from examples placed in its prompt, but it scored only 4% on Natural Questions (NQ) and 55 F1 on CoQA, far below fine-tuned systems. The open question was whether scaling the model enough could make prompting competitive.

2 · HOW — 3 STEPS

  1. Architecture: The team trained Generative Pre-trained Transformer 2 (GPT-2)-style decoder models from 125 million to 175 billion parameters. The largest model has 96 layers, width 12,288, and 96 attention heads; the context window is 2,048 tokens. Dense attention alternates with banded sparse attention, and the network uses pre-normalization.
  2. Training: The models see about 300 billion tokens drawn from Common Crawl (410 billion candidate tokens, weighted at 60% after filtering and deduplication of 45 terabytes), WebText2 (19 billion), Books (67 billion), and Wikipedia (3 billion). They are trained to predict the next token using Adam, a cosine learning-rate schedule, and model parallelism across V100 graphics processors.
  3. Inference: No weights are updated for a new task. Instead, the prompt contains 10–100 demonstrations followed by the query. Multiple-choice answers are selected using length-normalized likelihood, while open-ended generation uses beam search with a width of four.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

Predicting the next word across a huge and varied text collection teaches GPT-3 to act as a powerful pattern completer. Examples in the prompt can show the model which pattern to continue, so it can infer the task from context without gradient updates. The authors’ explanation is that spare model capacity supports this latent task inference, and that the useful amount of information supplied by a few examples increases sharply with model scale.

P(answer | instruction, demos, query; frozen θ)
In plain terms, the model stays frozen and uses the instruction, examples, and question in the prompt to choose a likely answer.
◍ Berkeley student Liam Porr used GPT-3 to write blog posts that attracted 26,000 readers and 60 new subscribers before revealing they were machine-written. The paper has 31 authors and openly discusses a decontamination bug, measuring test overlap with 13-gram matching.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE THE PAPER: THE LABEL FACTORY AND ITS THREE BILLS

  • Step 1 — What the game is: Natural language tasks — translate, answer questions, classify sentiment, fix grammar, generate examples. Before 2020 each task meant its own labeled dataset and its own trained model. You should care because the alternative — one model that takes a brand-new task as plain text — is exactly the interface behind every chatbot prompt window you have used since. The era’s habit: collect thousands of labels, fine-tune, publish a score, repeat for the next benchmark.
  • Step 2 — The words you need: An autoregressive language model predicts the next token from all previous tokens — left-looking during training, yet straightforward for sampling and likelihood scoring. Fine-tuning updates weights on thousands of labeled examples; in-context learning does the opposite: adaptation happens inside the forward pass by attending to demonstrations in the context window, with no gradient updates at all. The window here is 2048 tokens. Zero-shot gives only instructions, one-shot adds a single demonstration, few-shot shows ten to a hundred solved examples. Closed-book question answering answers facts from parameters alone, with no retriever.
  • Step 3 — How it worked before: The dominant recipe was pre-train on large text, then fine-tune per task — BERT (2) proved one pre-trained Transformer plus a small head can dominate eleven benchmarks. Meta-learning offered hints: GPT-2 suggested a model might adapt from its prompt alone, but it reached only 4% on Natural Questions and 55 F1 on CoQA, far behind fine-tuned systems. Meanwhile scaling laws showed smooth power-law trends in language-modeling loss, raising one question: would sheer scale make in-context learning competitive?
  • Step 4 — Where it broke: Three bills came due. Collecting a large labeled dataset per task is expensive and impractical across the huge range of useful asks, like correcting grammar or critiquing a story. Fine-tuning on a narrow distribution exploits spurious correlations and generalizes poorly — human-level benchmark scores can exaggerate real understanding. And humans learn differently: from one instruction, “tell me if this sentence is happy or sad,” or a single example, switching tasks fluidly mid-conversation. The label factory could not do that.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — THE IDEA, BUILT PIECE BY PIECE: LEARN ONCE, PERFORM FROM THE PROMPT

  • Step 5 — Don’t retrain, just prompt: Predict the next word at enormous scale and the model implicitly learns many skills — then trigger any of them by writing the task and a few solved examples into the prompt, updating no weights. The analogy: a brilliant improv actor who has read almost the entire internet. You state the rules once, act out a round or two, and the actor instantly plays the next round by recognizing the pattern. Few-shot means ten to a hundred rounds on stage within 2048 tokens; one-shot is one demonstration plus instructions; zero-shot is instructions only.
  • Step 6 — The architecture: A GPT-2 style autoregressive Transformer decoder, with modified initialization, pre-normalization, reversible tokenization, and alternating dense and locally banded sparse attention patterns to handle long contexts efficiently. The team trained eight sizes, from 125M parameters up to 175B — the full GPT-3 has 96 layers, width 12288, 96 attention heads of dimension 128, and a 2048-token window. Because it is a decoder with causal masking, every token attends only leftward — the mirror image of BERT’s two-way encoder, and exactly what next-word prediction requires.
  • Step 7 — The training recipe, decoded: The objective is plain autoregressive language modeling: at every position, predict the next token given all previous tokens — no task-specific head, so the loss simply asks, word by word, which token comes next given the history. Training blends quality-filtered, de-duplicated sources: filtered Common Crawl contributes 410B tokens at 60% sampling weight, plus WebText2, two book sets, and Wikipedia at 3% weight but 3.4 epochs. High-quality sets are deliberately over-weighted, accepting slight overfitting for better data; a classifier scored web pages against reference text and MinHash removed near-duplicates.
  • Step 8 — Using it with zero retraining: Tasks are specified purely as text. Few-shot places K solved examples plus one unsolved query in the window (K typically 10–100 to fit); one-shot gives one demonstration plus a natural-language description; zero-shot gives the description alone. For multiple choice the model compares the language-model likelihood of each completion given the context, often normalized per token for length, with options given meaningful names like True and False. Free-form answers continue the pattern with beam search width 4 — the entire adaptation happens in a forward pass.
  • Step 9 — Walk one query through: Suppose you want two-digit addition. The prompt holds a dozen lines like Q: 48 + 76 A: 124 and ends with an unsolved Q: 37 + 59 A:. Nothing is trained — the forward pass attends over the demonstrations and continues the pattern, and few-shot GPT-3 nails 100% of two-digit additions (while managing only 21% on composite single-digit expressions, a real limit). Reframe LAMBADA as fill-in-the-blank — Alice was friends with Bob → Bob — and accuracy leaps to 86.4% versus 76.2% zero-shot, showing framing itself matters.

STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — THE PROOF, THE LIMITS, AND WHAT IT UNLOCKED

  • Step 10 — What the numbers proved: Closed-book TriviaQA: 64.3% zero-shot already sits 14 points above fine-tuned T5-11B, and 71.2% few-shot sets a record with no retrieval at all. SuperGLUE: 71.8 few-shot versus 69 for fine-tuned BERT large, winning four of eight tasks with zero weight updates. LAMBADA: 86.4% few-shot versus 68% prior best. Humans detected 200-word GPT-3 news articles only 52% of the time. Yet RACE high school languishes at 46.8% against a 90% record — scale is powerful but uneven.
  • Step 11 — Why it works: Next-word prediction over hundreds of billions of tokens forces grammar, facts, style, and task structure to be absorbed as side effects of a single objective, and smooth power laws mean extra capacity keeps converting into skill. In-context learning is not hidden retraining: demonstrations become activations the forward pass conditions on — the outer loop of pre-training implicitly learns how to learn from prompts. Larger models use those on-stage hints more efficiently, which is why few-shot gains grow disproportionately with size.
  • Step 12 — Honest limits: The paper documents clear weaknesses: sentence-pair comparison like WiC sits at chance, adversarial NLI barely stirs, and structured reading like QuAC and RACE lag badly. Generation repeats itself, contradicts facts, and loses coherence over long passages; every token is weighted equally with no grounding in the physical world. Sample efficiency is poor — pre-training sees more text than any human lifetime. Inference at 175B parameters is expensive, internet biases persist, and a decontamination bug left some test overlaps, requiring clean-subset analysis.
  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: GPT-3 made prompting the core interface for language models. InstructGPT (8) fine-tunes it with human feedback to follow instructions reliably — the road to ChatGPT — and Chain-of-Thought (9) prompts the same decoders to reason step by step. Chinchilla (10) shows the recipe was undertrained and fixes its compute trade-offs. LoRA (11) makes fine-tuning at this scale affordable, FlashAttention (12) makes long contexts fast, and LLaMA 2 (13), DPO (14), DeepSeek-R1 (15) open, align, and teach reasoning to these decoders. RAG (4), CLIP (6), and Latent Diffusion (7) share the scale-and-prompt era it defined.

Chinchilla — feed, don’t fatten (20 tok/param)

1 · PROBLEM — STARVED GIANTS

Kaplan and colleagues’ 2020 scaling recommendation spent each tenfold increase in compute on about 5.5 times as many parameters but only 1.8 times as much training data. That recipe produced models such as GPT-3 (175 billion parameters trained on 300 billion tokens), Gopher (280 billion on 300 billion), and MT-NLG (530 billion on 270 billion). These models had enormous capacity but too few examples to use it fully, wasting both training compute and the much larger lifetime cost of serving an oversized model. The field kept enlarging the model instead of giving it enough data.

2 · HOW — 3 ESTIMATES AGREE

  1. Loss law: The authors model loss as L(N,D)=E+A/Nα+B/Dβ, subject to a training-compute budget of 6ND=C. Here N is the number of parameters, D is the amount of data, and E is irreducible entropy.
  2. Three fits: They compare fixed-size models trained for different durations across 70 million–10 billion parameters and four data exposures; fit nine compute-matched IsoFLOP loss curves spanning 6e18–3e21 FLOPs; and fit the Huber-loss/L-BFGS model with E=1.69, α=0.34, and β=0.28. All three analyses predict N∝C~0.5 and D∝C~0.5.
  3. Proof build: They train Chinchilla with 70 billion parameters on 1.4 trillion tokens. It has 80 layers and width 8,192, uses AdamW and a compute-matched cosine schedule, and grows its batch size from 1.5 million to 3 million tokens. It is trained on MassiveText for the same total FLOPs as Gopher.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

Model capacity and training data need to grow together: roughly doubling the parameters calls for doubling the training tokens. Earlier experiments used schedules that stopped large models before they had fully benefited from their compute, which biased the recommendation toward bigger networks. With compute-matched schedules, the experiments show that a well-trained, data-rich smaller model can outperform a starved giant at the same training cost and then remain cheaper every time it is used.

N* ∝ C^0.5 · D* ∝ C^0.5 · ~20 tok/param
In plain terms, divide additional compute roughly evenly between adding model parameters and providing more training tokens, at about 20 tokens per parameter.
◍ Chinchilla’s name is a rodent joke: DeepMind followed its larger Gopher model with a smaller animal that could win when trained on enough data. The paper received an Outstanding Paper award at NeurIPS 2022, and its authors said PaLM’s wins were expected because it used about five times more compute overall.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE THE PAPER: GIANT MODELS ON A 300B-TOKEN DIET

  • Step 1 — What the game is: Training large language models costs real money and electricity, and every lab faces one budget question: with this many computer operations available, build a bigger model or feed it more text? You should care because this single decision sets both what your chatbot knows and what every answer costs to generate afterward. The currency is FLOPs — floating-point operations, literally a count of the basic math that training performs, treated as a fixed budget to spend.
  • Step 2 — The words you need: Parameters, written N, are the adjustable knobs inside the model: more knobs remember more patterns, but each costs compute to train and to run. Tokens, written D, are the word-pieces the model reads — 1.4 trillion tokens is roughly a trillion words of books, web pages, code, and news. Training loss measures how surprised the model is by real text; lower is better. An IsoFLOP curve compares model sizes that all cost the same total FLOPs, and a power law is a straight line on log-log axes used to predict far beyond measured runs.
  • Step 3 — How it worked before: The field followed Kaplan’s 2020 scaling advice: with 10× more compute, grow parameters about 5.5× but training words only about 1.8×. So nearly every big model trained on roughly 300B tokens regardless of size — GPT-3 (3) at 175B parameters, Gopher at 280B, Megatron-Turing NLG at 530B, Jurassic-1 at 178B, LaMDA at 137B, all on 168–300B tokens. Bigger was assumed strictly better, and data was treated as a constant of nature.
  • Step 4 — Where it broke: Every one of those giants was under-trained — like very large students who only read a few textbooks, wasting their size because they had not seen enough examples to learn well. With GPT-3 (3) as poster child (175B parameters on 300B tokens), the field kept hiring cooks while the grocery list stayed fixed. Benchmarks kept rising, so nobody questioned the recipe — yet the same compute was clearly not being spent optimally, and every one of those models also cost a fortune to serve.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — THE IDEA, BUILT PIECE BY PIECE: SPLIT THE COMPUTE EVENLY

  • Step 5 — Feed, don’t fatten: Model size and training words should grow together in equal steps — double the parameters and double the tokens. Given 10× more compute, make the model about 3× bigger and feed it about 3× more words, not 5.5× bigger on the same data. The kitchen analogy: Kaplan kept hiring cooks while buying the same food, leaving a crowded kitchen with nothing to chop; Chinchilla hires and shops together so every cook stays busy. The memorable rule of thumb: about 20 training tokens per parameter.
  • Step 6 — The question, made mathematical: Choose N and D to minimize training loss L(N,D) subject to a fixed budget C, where total FLOPs ≈ 6 × N × D, so size and data trade off directly. Across 400+ trained models (70M to 16B+ parameters, 5B to 400B+ tokens), three approaches attacked the trade-off. Approach one fixed size and varied training length, fitting power laws through the best points: best size ∝ C^0.50, best data ∝ C^0.50. Approach two fixed the budget — each IsoFLOP plot is a bowl with one lowest point, giving exponents 0.49 and 0.51. Both ways: equal square-root scaling.
  • Step 7 — The third approach, decoded: Fit one formula to every run: L(N,D) = E + A/N^α + B/D^β. E is the unavoidable confusion of natural text itself — the randomness even a perfect writer cannot remove, fitted ≈ 1.69. A/N^α is the imitation gap a model with only N knobs cannot close, shrinking as N grows (A ≈ 406, α ≈ 0.34). B/D^β is the under-training gap that shrinks as D grows (B ≈ 411, β ≈ 0.28). Fitted robustly with Huber loss and L-BFGS, the pencil-and-paper optimum gives exponents 0.46 and 0.54 — again nearly equal scaling.
  • Step 8 — The detail everyone missed: The cosine learning-rate schedule — the rate starts high and falls gently like a hill — must be matched to the number of tokens you actually train on. Stop early against a long schedule and your loss looks worse than it should — part of why Kaplan over-favored big models; in good runs the rate decays about 10× over the real token count. With that fixed, Chinchilla trained 70B parameters (80 layers, width 8192, 64 heads) on 1.4T tokens with AdamW, batch growing 1.5M → 3M tokens, on TPUv3 and TPUv4 chips.
  • Step 9 — Walk the budget arithmetic: Gopher spent a budget of roughly 280B parameters × 300B tokens, and since FLOPs ≈ 6ND, Chinchilla answers with 70B × 1.4T ≈ the same spend — the 6ND products nearly match. Check the rule: 70B parameters × 20 tokens = 1.4T tokens, exactly the prescription. Four times fewer knobs, about 4.7× more text, same training compute. The payoff shows up immediately: on MMLU’s 57 exam subjects, Chinchilla beat Gopher on 51, tied on 2, and lost only 4 — college mathematics, econometrics, moral scenarios, formal logic. The smaller, better-fed model wins.

STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — THE PROOF, THE LIMITS, AND WHAT IT UNLOCKED

  • Step 10 — What the numbers proved: MMLU: 67.6% five-shot for Chinchilla versus 60.0 for Gopher, 43.9 for GPT-3, and 34.5 for average human raters. On the Pile it scored lower bits-per-byte than Gopher on every single subset; Wikitext103 perplexity 7.16 versus 7.75. LAMBADA 77.4 versus Gopher’s 74.5; RACE-hard 82.3 versus 71.6, a gain of more than ten points. BIG-bench averaged 65.1 versus 54.4. TruthfulQA jumped from 29.5 to 43.6. New closed-book records on Natural Questions — and being 4× smaller, every answer Chinchilla ever serves is cheaper.
  • Step 11 — Why it works: Parameters are capacity, but data is what fills it — an oversized model on too few tokens has knobs it never gets to train, wasting both memory and math. The equal-scaling law falls straight out of the loss fit: the parameter gap shrinks like N^-0.34 and the data gap like D^-0.28, and those similar exponents mean similar dollars buy similar gains on either side. And training cost is paid once while inference cost is paid on every query, so a smaller, smarter model saves compute for its whole lifetime.
  • Step 12 — Honest limits: Only two truly large runs exist — Gopher and Chinchilla — and the other 400+ are much smaller, so trillion-parameter predictions are far extrapolations beyond direct measurement. The clean power-law story curves slightly at high budgets, hinting that even smaller models might win there. Every scaling run uses less than one full pass over the data, so repeated epochs are unstudied. And 1.4T-token scrapes carry more of everything: Chinchilla still shows Winogender bias and about the same toxicity rate as Gopher — better training does not automatically fix safety.
  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: Chinchilla ended the era of 300B-token training. LLaMA 2 (13) explicitly adopts the recipe — its 70B model trains on 2T tokens, very close to the 20-per-parameter rule — and DeepSeek-R1 (15) carries the data-rich philosophy into reasoning. InstructGPT (8) and DPO (14) fine-tune Chinchilla-optimal base models that are cheaper to train and to serve, and Chain-of-Thought (9) reasoning is more practical on such models. It builds directly on GPT-3 (3) — the recipe it corrects — and on the Transformers of Attention Is All You Need (1) and the pre-training power BERT (2) proved.

RAG — don’t memorize the world, retrieve it

1 · PROBLEM — FROZEN PARROT

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) addresses the limits of closed-book question answering with systems such as T5-11B, which stored facts in its weights and reached 34.5 exact match on Natural Questions (NQ). Those facts could become outdated, the model could not point to sources, and it sometimes generated fluent false answers. Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) reached 41.5 and could retrieve evidence, but its extractive answers had to copy a matching text span; a changed phrasing or a question needing evidence from multiple documents could defeat it. There was no general sequence-to-sequence method for generating fluent answers grounded in retrieved evidence.

2 · HOW — 3 STEPS

  1. Architecture: A Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) system uses separate BERT-base encoders for questions and documents, scores them with a dot product, and searches a FAISS maximum-inner-product index containing 21 million 100-word Wikipedia passages from December 2018. A 400-million-parameter BART-large generator receives the question together with a retrieved passage.
  2. Variants: RAG-Sequence selects one retrieved document for a complete generated answer and marginalizes over documents using p(y|x)=Σzp(z|x)p(y|x,z). RAG-Token instead mixes retrieved-document probabilities for each generated token, which allows evidence to be combined across documents.
  3. Training and decoding: The retriever and generator are trained end to end with negative log-likelihood marginalized over the top K documents. The document encoder and index stay frozen, so the index is not rebuilt; the question encoder and BART are tuned. At inference, the system decodes a beam for each document and then re-scores the candidates.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

The design gives the generator two complementary kinds of memory: its learned parameters provide fluent language, while retrieved passages supply updateable factual evidence. Marginalizing over several documents lets the answer account for uncertainty in the retriever instead of trusting only its top result. When facts change, the index can be replaced without retraining the language model. Retrieval and generation therefore cooperate: one finds evidence and the other explains it.

p(y|x) = Σz∈topK p(z|x) · p(y|x,z)
In plain terms, the model weighs several relevant documents and combines their evidence when generating an answer.
◍ In a world-leader experiment, matching 2016 and 2018 Wikipedia indexes gave about 70% and 68% accuracy, respectively, while mismatched indexes dropped to 12% and 4%. The authors also released an early interactive demo through Hugging Face Transformers to show how updating the index could refresh answers.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE THE PAPER: A GENIUS WITH A FROZEN, UNVERIFIABLE MEMORY

  • Step 1 — What the problem is: Open-domain question answering is the job of answering factual questions from a huge, unlabeled pile of text rather than a small labeled set. Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, gives a language model an open book: it finds relevant documents and writes an answer grounded in them. You should care because this is the difference between an assistant whose knowledge can be updated by replacing a document and one whose knowledge is frozen until someone pays for another training run.
  • Step 2 — The words you need: Parametric memory is knowledge baked into network weights, like what you remember after studying; it is powerful but hard to edit. Non-parametric memory is knowledge stored outside the weights as searchable text, like books on a shelf; it is easy to inspect and replace. Dense Passage Retrieval, or DPR, uses two BERT encoders to map questions and passages into vectors and scores them by dot product. Maximum Inner Product Search, or MIPS, finds the top vectors fast, using a FAISS index. RAG-Sequence blames one passage for a whole answer; RAG-Token lets different words come from different passages.
  • Step 3 — How it worked before: The ambitious route was closed-book: train a giant model until facts live in its parameters. T5 with 11 billion parameters answered Natural Questions from memory alone and scored 34.5 Exact Match, while GPT-3-style models showed that scale stores enormous amounts of knowledge. The other route was extractive retrieval: DPR, REALM, and ORQA found passages, but the model could only copy a short span of text out of them. Neither route combined free-form generation with a searchable, editable memory.
  • Step 4 — Where it broke: Three failures made this painful. Knowledge froze at training time, so the model could not learn that a new president had been elected. It could not cite sources, so users could not check an answer. And it hallucinated — fluent, confident sentences that were factually wrong. Extractive systems had their own cliff: if no retrieved document contained the exact answer string, they scored zero, and they could not fuse clues spread across several documents. RAG attacks all four problems at once.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — THE OPEN-BOOK MACHINE: RETRIEVE, READ, WRITE

  • Step 5 — The open-book exam: A closed-book student must memorize every fact and restudy when the textbook changes; an open-book student only learns how to find the right page and explain it clearly. BART is the student’s writing and reasoning ability, the Wikipedia index is the library, and DPR is the librarian who fetches useful books. If the world changes you hot-swap the index instead of retraining the model. The bet is that a modest reader-writer plus good retrieval can beat a giant memorizer.
  • Step 6 — The architecture: Two learned parts cooperate. The retriever runs two BERT-base encoders, one for the question and one for each passage; the score is their dot product, which measures whether question and passage point in a similar direction. Passage vectors are precomputed into a FAISS index over 21 million disjoint 100-word Wikipedia chunks and searched by MIPS. The generator is BART-large, a 400-million-parameter encoder-decoder Transformer. To use a passage, the system simply concatenates the question with it and feeds the combined text into BART.
  • Step 7 — The training objective, decoded: Training is end-to-end without ever telling the retriever which document is correct. The objective is the negative log-likelihood of the right answer after marginalizing over documents: p(answer | question) = Σ p(passage | question) · p(answer | question, passage). In words, each retrieved passage makes a guess, passages are weighted by how promising the retriever thought they were, and the model is punished when that weighted mixture assigns low probability to the true answer. Only the question encoder and BART are fine-tuned; the document encoder and the 21-million-vector index stay frozen, so training never rebuilds the index.
  • Step 8 — What happens at test time: The two variants decode differently. RAG-Token mixes passage predictions at every token, so it looks like an ordinary next-token distribution and plain beam search works. RAG-Sequence commits one passage to the entire answer, so its probability does not factor per token; the paper runs beam search separately for each document and re-scores the candidates by summing across documents. The exact version is Thorough Decoding; Fast Decoding skips extra forward passes. Retrieving more documents keeps helping RAG-Sequence, while RAG-Token peaks around ten.
  • Step 9 — One question, walked through: Take a Jeopardy question that mentions two different novels by the same author. The question encoder turns it into one vector; FAISS returns the top K passages, say one about the first novel and one about the second. RAG-Token feeds question-plus-passage-one and question-plus-passage-two into BART and mixes their next-token distributions at every position, so the answer can take the author from one source and the second title from the other. RAG-Sequence instead commits one passage to the whole answer — fine for simple lookups, weaker when two facts must be fused.

STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — THE PROOF, THE LIMITS, AND THE LIBRARY CARD

  • Step 10 — What the numbers proved: On Natural Questions, RAG-Sequence hit 44.5 Exact Match, beating DPR at 41.5, REALM at 40.4, and closed-book T5-11B at 34.5 — a small open-book system beating one eleven times larger. On TriviaQA’s Wikipedia split it reached 68.0, and on WebQuestions 45.5 versus DPR’s 41.1. On Natural Questions it still answered 11.8 percent correctly when no retrieved document contained the exact answer, where extractive systems score zero. Human judges called RAG more factual in 42.7 percent of comparisons and BART in only 7.1 percent; FEVER reached 72.5 percent three-way and 89.5 percent two-way.
  • Step 11 — Why the design works: Retrieval and generation cover each other’s weaknesses. Parametric memory supplies fluency, reasoning, and common sense; non-parametric memory supplies freshness, specificity, and citations. Because the question encoder is trained through the generator’s loss, retrieval is shaped toward passages that actually help answer, not merely passages that look similar — ablations show frozen-retriever and BM25 variants degrade, especially on open-domain QA. Marginalization is a principled hedge: when the model cannot tell which source is best, it listens to several and lets their votes average out.
  • Step 12 — Honest limits: The system’s world is Wikipedia. If the answer is absent, biased, or outdated, RAG can still fail, and some MS-MARCO questions force it to fall back on parametric guessing. The frozen document encoder means the index never improves during fine-tuning, and on story generation the retriever can collapse — returning the same documents for every input until the generator ignores them and RAG degrades to plain BART. Inference is more expensive too, since every retrieved document needs its own generation pass, and FEVER still trails heavily engineered pipelines when strong evidence supervision is available.
  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: RAG gave the field its name and its standard recipe. Retrieval-augmented generation is now an industry category, and every assistant that searches your documents or cites sources descends from it. InstructGPT (08) and LLaMA 2 (13) built powerful chat models that still freeze in time and hallucinate; RAG is the complementary grounding layer. DeepSeek-R1 (15) reasons better, but reasoning needs correct facts. LoRA (11) makes the generator and query-encoder fine-tuning cheaper. Attention (01), BERT (02), and GPT-3 (03) built stronger readers and writers; RAG gave them a library card.
2020 · Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models · Ho et al., Berkeley · NeurIPS 2020 Read Paper 05 notes

DDPM — destroy with noise, learn to undestroy

1 · PROBLEM — GAN LOTTERY

Image generation in 2020 involved difficult trade-offs. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) could make sharp pictures, but training was unstable and mode collapse could make them repeat only a few kinds of faces. Autoregressive models had strong likelihoods but generated pixels in sequence, taking O(n) steps; normalizing flows and variational autoencoders (VAEs) often produced blurry images. Diffusion had been proposed by Sohl-Dickstein in 2015, but its samples were not yet competitive, while score-based and energy-based model (EBM) samplers needed manually designed annealing schedules. The goal was stable regression training with image quality comparable to GANs.

2 · HOW — 3 STEPS

  1. Architecture: A U-Net combines ideas from PixelCNN++, Wide ResNets, Group Normalization, 16×16 attention, and sinusoidal timestep embeddings like those in the Transformer. The model has 35 million parameters for CIFAR and 114–256 million for LSUN. It uses 1,000 diffusion steps and a linear noise schedule with β increasing from 1e-4 to 0.02; images are scaled to [−1,1].
  2. Training: A fixed forward process adds Gaussian noise according to q(xt|x0)=N(√ᾱx0,(1−ᾱ)I), so any noise level can be sampled directly in one step. The network learns the reverse process by predicting the added noise ε, minimizing Lsimple=E||ε−εθ(xt,t)||² with timesteps sampled uniformly.
  3. Sampling: Generation begins with x sampled from N(0,I), then repeatedly estimates xt−1 from the current noisy image using the learned reverse process and adds σz, with z set to zero at the final step. Early reverse steps establish the image’s broad composition and later steps add texture.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

Predicting the added noise residual is a well-conditioned task compared with predicting every clean pixel directly. Giving noise levels uniform training weight prevents the model from spending most of its capacity on nearly clean images and imperceptible details. At generation time, the 1,000 small denoising decisions compose into a sharp and varied image distribution. Stable regression avoids the adversarial instability of GAN training while covering more modes.

Lsimple = Et,ε || ε − εθ(xt,t) ||²
In plain terms, the model practices at every noise level and learns to identify the noise added to each image.
◍ DDPM revived the 2015 diffusion formulation, whose thermodynamics-inspired mathematics had not yet produced samples competitive with GANs. The winning recipe was notably simple, and the paper reports that using exponential-moving-average weights improved FID by well over a point.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE THE PAPER: THE GAN EMPIRE AND ITS BLIND SPOT

  • Step 1 — What image generation is: Image generation means producing new pictures that look like they came from a real dataset. Diffusion models now power Stable Diffusion, Midjourney, and DALL-E, but the recipe that made them work was not obvious. DDPM, Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models, is the paper that proved a network can learn to reverse noise and match the sharpness of state-of-the-art GANs while training as stably as ordinary regression. If you want to understand why modern image AI is stable rather than a fragile adversarial duel, start here.
  • Step 2 — The words you need: The forward process is a fixed chain that adds Gaussian noise until an image becomes static; the reverse process is the learned chain that removes it, one Gaussian step at a time. The noise schedule, written as β values, fixes how much noise each of the 1000 steps adds. Epsilon prediction asks the network to output the noise that was added rather than the clean image, and L-simple is the average squared error between true and predicted noise. FID and Inception score measure sample quality with an Inception classifier; lower FID and higher Inception are better.
  • Step 3 — How it worked before: Before 2020, generation belonged to GANs. A generator and a discriminator fought: the discriminator judged real versus fake, and the generator learned to fool it. GANs produced sharp images but were unstable to train and prone to mode collapse, repeating a few convincing faces while ignoring the rest of the distribution. Autoregressive models had excellent likelihoods but generated pixel by pixel in a fixed order, so they were slow. Diffusion itself was not new — Sohl-Dickstein proposed the thermodynamics-flavoured math in 2015 — but its samples were nowhere near competitive.
  • Step 4 — Where it broke: The pain point was a bad trade: quality or stability, rarely both. GAN training could collapse or oscillate, and score-based samplers needed hand-tuned annealing after training. Diffusion had a principled forward-and-reverse formulation, and nobody could make it produce good pictures. The field needed proof that a diffusion model could be simple to train, stable like regression, and still competitive on perceptual quality with BigGAN and StyleGAN. That is exactly the gap DDPM filled, with two small but decisive choices.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — THE MECHANISM: DESTROY WITH NOISE, LEARN TO UNDESTROY

  • Step 5 — The central insight: Imagine a drop of ink spreading in water. After many steps the water is uniformly gray and all memory of the drop seems lost; learn to play the film backwards and a sharp drop reappears from static. DDPM learns that backwards movie. The two decisive choices are to train the network to predict the noise that was added — not the clean image — and to use a plain unweighted squared-error loss. Noise prediction is a numerically calm target, and the simple loss spends equal practice on light and heavy fog.
  • Step 6 — The forward chain, decoded: The forward chain is fixed and has no learned parameters. At each step the image is shrunken slightly and fresh Gaussian noise is added: q(x_t | x_{t−1}) is a Gaussian centred on √(1 − β_t) · x_{t−1} with variance β_t. Because Gaussians compose, you can jump to any level directly: x_t = √ᾱₜ · x₀ + √(1 − ᾱₜ) · ε, where x₀ is the original image, ε is standard normal noise, and ᾱₜ is the product of all (1 − β) values up to step t. Tell me the photo and the noise level, and I can generate the correctly fogged version instantly.
  • Step 7 — The training objective, decoded: The reverse chain starts from pure noise and applies Gaussian steps whose means are learned; the variances are fixed constants. The key parameterization predicts ε: instead of painting the clean photo, a hard and unstable target, the network says what grain was just added, and subtracting a scaled version of that prediction steps toward a cleaner image. The exact variational bound weights time steps unevenly, over-emphasizing nearly clean images; the authors drop that weighting. L-simple picks a random image, a random step, and random noise, forms x_t, and penalizes (ε − ε_θ(x_t, t))² — the squared distance between true and predicted noise.
  • Step 8 — Architecture and inference: The backbone is a U-Net in the PixelCNN++ style: Wide ResNet blocks, group normalization, self-attention at the 16×16 resolution, and sinusoidal time embeddings — borrowed from Transformers — injected into every residual block so one network handles all 1000 noise levels. CIFAR-10 uses about 36 million parameters; the 256×256 models about 114 million. Sampling starts from random Gaussian noise and iterates backwards: predict the noise, shift the image toward a cleaner version using the schedule, and add a little fresh noise except at the final step. Early steps set pose and layout; late steps add hair strands and texture.
  • Step 9 — One image, walked through: Take one CIFAR-10 image and noise level t = 500. Training picks that image, samples ε from a normal distribution, and computes x₅₀₀ = √ᾱ₅₀₀ · x₀ + √(1 − ᾱ₅₀₀) · ε, so the picture is mostly static with a faint ghost of the original. The network receives x₅₀₀ and the number 500 and predicts ε̂. The loss pushes ε̂ toward the actual ε that was used. At sampling time the same prediction is subtracted, scaled by the schedule, to produce x₄₉₉ — then the loop repeats, each step sharpening the ghost until a full image emerges.

STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — THE PROOF, THE LIMITS, AND THE DIFFUSION DECADE

  • Step 10 — What the numbers proved: On unconditional CIFAR-10 the simplified-objective model reached an Inception score of 9.46 and an FID of 3.17 against the training set — 5.24 against the test set — beating BigGAN at 14.73, score-based models in the 25 to 38 range, and PixelCNN variants near 50 to 65, and nearly matching StyleGAN2 with adaptive augmentation at 3.26 without any adversarial training. The ablation is the punchline: posterior-mean prediction with the true bound scored 13.22 FID, noise prediction with the true bound 13.51, and noise prediction with the simple loss 3.17 — roughly a fourfold improvement from combining both choices.
  • Step 11 — Why the design works: Denoising is a thousand tiny regression problems, not one adversarial game, so gradients are well behaved and training never collapses. Predicting noise rather than pixels makes each target approximately standard normal, which is easy to fit at every noise level. The unweighted loss is the second half: by practising equally at light, medium, and heavy fog, the network spends capacity where perception actually changes instead of on invisible near-clean details. Weight averaging and a large receptive-field U-Net add polish; the paper reports that sampling from moving-averaged weights alone improves FID by well over a point.
  • Step 12 — Honest limits: Sampling is slow: one batch needs 1000 sequential network evaluations, so a batch of 256 CIFAR images takes many seconds and 256×256 samples take minutes. CLIP — language programs vision (400M pairs)

1 · PROBLEM — FIXED VOCAB PRISON

Conventional image classifiers needed a new labelled dataset, output head, and fine-tuning run for every new concept. Earlier caption-based learning had not solved the problem: Visual N-Grams reached only 11.5% zero-shot accuracy on ImageNet, compared with roughly 50% for a classic classifier and 88% for a supervised model. Hashtag and other weak-label pretraining still produced fixed classifiers, so they could not freely recognize concepts expressed as text or transfer reliably to optical character recognition (OCR), actions, and geography.

2 · HOW — 3 STEPS

  1. Data, WebImageText (WIT): Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining (CLIP) uses 400 million image–text pairs collected through 500,000 Wikipedia and WordNet queries, with at most 20,000 pairs per query. This is larger than the 100,000 examples in COCO and the 15 million images in YFCC, and its text contains roughly as many words as GPT-2’s WebText.
  2. Model and objective: An image encoder based on a ResNet or Vision Transformer (ViT-B or ViT-L) is paired with a 63-million-parameter, 12-layer text Transformer with a 76-token byte-pair encoding (BPE) context. Both encoders produce L2-normalized vectors in a shared space. A batch of 32,768 image–caption pairs is trained with a bidirectional contrastive objective using cosine similarity and a temperature parameter.
  3. Inference: For each candidate class, the model embeds a prompt such as “a photo of a {label}” and chooses the class whose text vector has the highest cosine similarity to the image vector. The paper also averages text embeddings from an ensemble of 80 prompts.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

Matching 400 million noisy image–caption pairs places open-vocabulary meaning in a shared visual and linguistic space, spanning objects, text in images, actions, and places. A natural-language description can therefore act as a classifier label: encode the description and compare it with the image. Using prompts that resemble the captions seen during training also reduces the mismatch between training and test inputs and improves accuracy.

L = −log exp(cos(I,T⁺)/τ) / Σ exp(cos/τ)
In plain terms, the training objective pulls each matching image and caption together and pushes it away from the other 32,767 captions in the batch.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE THE PAPER: VISION LOCKED INTO A THOUSAND CHECKBOXES

  • Step 1 — What the problem is: Computer vision classification means teaching a machine to name what is in a picture. For a decade that meant training on a fixed list of categories, usually ImageNet’s one thousand classes. CLIP, Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training, replaced the list with language: train on 400 million internet image-caption pairs and learn to match them. You should care because CLIP made words the interface to vision — it is why you can type a prompt and get an image, and why a model can recognize a concept it was never explicitly trained to label.
  • Step 2 — The words you need: Contrastive learning means learning by comparison: pull true image-text pairs together in a shared embedding space and push mismatched pairs apart, no category labels needed. Zero-shot transfer means using the model on a new dataset with no extra training, just by describing the classes in words. A linear probe freezes the encoder and trains a simple logistic classifier on its features, measuring representation quality. Temperature scaling divides similarity scores by a learned softness knob before softmax. WIT, WebImageText, is the 400-million-pair dataset. Distribution shift means testing on images that look different — sketches, video frames, renditions.
  • Step 3 — How it worked before: Earlier work was stuck in one classroom. A typical classifier trained on a fixed label set, so recognizing a new concept meant collecting new labeled images, adding an output layer, and fine-tuning. Caption-based attempts such as Visual N-Grams reached only 11.5 percent zero-shot on ImageNet, against roughly 88 percent for the best fully supervised models. Systems built on hashtags or noisy label sets still ended with a static classifier vocabulary. Nothing had scaled the idea that natural language itself should be the supervision signal.
  • Step 4 — Where it broke: The concrete pain was re-labeling. Suppose you wanted a model to recognize twenty species of orchid. You would pay annotators, collect thousands of images, and retrain — and next month the same expense for mushrooms. Meanwhile the internet already contains billions of pictures with captions that describe objects, actions, and text inside the image, for free. The bottleneck was not data; it was the fixed-softmax recipe that could not turn free-form language into a usable classifier. That is precisely what CLIP fixes.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — THE MATCHING GAME: ONE ROOM FOR PIXELS AND WORDS

  • Step 5 — The central insight: Picture a party game: a table of photos, a table of captions, and your job is to reunite each photo with its true caption. Get good at that game across hundreds of millions of examples and you must have learned what dogs, maps, and Eiffel Towers look like, even how to read text inside images. CLIP trains exactly this game. Image and text encoders map both into one shared room; true pairs are pulled together, wrong pairs pushed apart. Classification later becomes matching: write a photo of a cat, embed it, and pick the nearest class sentence.
  • Step 6 — The data and the two towers: WIT contains 400 million image-text pairs harvested from the public internet using 500,000 queries drawn from Wikipedia words, bigrams, titles, and WordNet, with up to 20,000 pairs per query — an order of magnitude larger and more diverse than filtered YFCC100M’s roughly 15 million usable English pairs. The image side studies ResNets with attention pooling, scaled to RN50x4 through RN50x64, plus Vision Transformers ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16, and ViT-L/14 with an extra 336-pixel fine-tune. The text side is a 63-million-parameter, 12-layer Transformer reading byte-pair tokens up to 76 long; the final end-token activation is the sentence vector.
  • Step 7 — The training objective, decoded: Given a batch of N true pairs, there are N² possible pairings and only N correct ones on the diagonal. Both encoders produce unit-length vectors; the model computes cosine similarities for every image-text combination, scales them by a learned temperature, and applies cross-entropy in both directions — given an image pick the right caption, given a caption pick the right image — then averages the two losses. In plain words, every batch is a 32,768-way multiple-choice quiz. Training used batch size 32,768, Adam with cosine decay, mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, and 32 epochs; the largest ResNet took 18 days on 592 V100 GPUs.
  • Step 8 — What happens at inference: Zero-shot inference precomputes a text embedding for each class description and classifies an image by cosine similarity followed by softmax. That is mathematically logistic regression with normalized inputs and weights, no bias, and temperature scaling — except the weights were written in language rather than learned from labeled images. Wording matters: the template a photo of a label improves ImageNet by 1.3 percent because training captions are sentences, and averaging 80 templates such as a photo of a big label adds another 3.5 percent. Task-specific prompts help too, like a type of pet on Oxford Pets.
  • Step 9 — One photo, walked through: Run a single test photo through ImageNet inference. The image encoder turns it into one vector. The text encoder has already embedded a thousand prompts such as a photo of a golden retriever and a photo of a tench. The model computes cosine similarity between the photo vector and every prompt vector — suppose the retriever prompt scores 0.31 and the fish prompt 0.22 — divides each by the learned temperature, and applies softmax. The top score becomes the prediction. No weight was updated; the classifier was printed on demand from words.

STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — ZERO-SHOT PROOF, HONEST FAILURES, AND THE PROMPT ERA

  • Step 10 — What the numbers proved: Zero-shot ImageNet accuracy rose from the prior caption-based baseline of 11.5 percent to 76.2 percent, matching a fully supervised ResNet-50 trained on 1.28 million labels. Yahoo rose from 72.4 to 98.4 percent and SUN scenes from 23.0 to 58.5. Across 27 datasets, zero-shot CLIP beat a supervised linear probe on ResNet-50 features on 16, hit 99.3 percent on STL-10, and gained over 20 points on Stanford Cars and Food101. It also cut the in-distribution to out-of-distribution gap by up to 75 percent on seven natural-shift benchmarks — yet specialized tasks like tumor detection or counting can lag supervised models by 10 to 35 points.
  • Step 11 — Why the design works: Language supplies an open vocabulary, and 400 million noisy pairs supply the diversity to use it. The contrastive batch is the engine: with 32,768 candidates, easy negatives are plentiful, so the model must encode fine distinctions — not just dog versus cat, but breed, pose, and scene. Prompts then align the test distribution with training: because captions were sentences, sentence-shaped class descriptions retrieve better answers. Robustness falls out of diversity too; seeing sketches, memes, and product photos on the internet teaches invariances that a clean labeled dataset never contains.
  • Step 12 — Honest limits: Fine-grained and systematic tasks remain hard: similar car models, flower species, and aircraft variants get confused, counting is poor, and MNIST accuracy is only 88 percent — worse than logistic regression on raw pixels, because handwriting barely appears in pre-training. The method is data-hungry in the extreme: 12.8 billion pair-views over 32 epochs, which at one image per second would take over 400 years. Prompt wording shifts results, so two developers can disagree using the same model. And zero-shot usually trails a fully supervised model on the same features by 10 to 25 points.
  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: CLIP made language the remote control for vision. Latent Diffusion (07) conditions denoising on CLIP text embeddings, and DDPM (05) supplies the noise loop — together they became Stable Diffusion. InstructGPT (08) and LLaMA 2 (13) extend the same prompt-and-scale philosophy to instruction following, while Chain-of-Thought (09) and DeepSeek-R1 (15) show that a flexible interface plus scale unlocks new tasks without retraining. In industry, CLIP became the vision bridge inside DALL-E 2, Flamingo, LLaVA, GPT-4V, open-vocabulary detection, and video retrieval.

Latent Diffusion — dream in sketch (→ Stable Diffusion)

1 · PROBLEM — PIXEL POVERTY

Generative adversarial networks (GANs) made sharp images but trained unstably and sometimes collapsed to a narrow set of outputs. Autoregressive (AR) Transformers needed billions of parameters and generated images token by token. Pixel-space denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) processed every H×W×3 pixel at every step, requiring 150–1,000 V100-days to train and about five A100-days to produce 50,000 samples. That cost made the systems difficult to access and energy-intensive, while the original diffusion setup did not provide text control. The challenge was to retain DDPM image quality while achieving the efficiency of the methods in Papers 11 and 12 and the language steering of Paper 06.

2 · HOW — 3 STEPS

  1. Compress: An encoder maps an image of size H×W×3 into a latent representation h×w×c, downsampling by a factor f of four to eight. It is trained with a combination of reconstruction, perceptual, patch-GAN, and small Kullback–Leibler (KL) or vector-quantization (VQ) losses, then frozen.
  2. Diffuse in the latent space: A 1,000-step noise process is applied to the compressed representation. A time-conditioned U-Net built from convolution and attention layers learns to predict the noise ε using mean squared error.
  3. Condition and sample: Spatial conditions can be concatenated with the latent, while text and layouts can guide generation through cross-attention using BERT or CLIP representations. Denoising diffusion implicit models (DDIM) reduce sampling to 50–250 steps, guidance uses a scale of 1.5, and the decoder expands the final latent back to an image once.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

Full-resolution pixels contain many small texture details that are expensive to process but do not all matter equally for meaning. The autoencoder learns to preserve perceptually important detail once, while diffusion composes the image’s semantics in a space 16–64 times smaller. Decoding only after denoising amortizes the cost of hundreds of diffusion steps, making image generation much cheaper without giving up the reported quality.

z=E(x) · L=E||ε−εθ(zt,t,c)||² · x̂=D(z0)
In plain terms, the encoder compresses the image, diffusion removes noise from its meaning-rich representation, and the decoder expands it back once.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE THE PAPER: BEAUTIFUL PICTURES, BRUTAL PRICE TAG

  • Step 1 — What the problem is: High-resolution text-to-image generation means turning a written prompt into a large, detailed picture. Latent Diffusion is the paper behind Stable Diffusion, the model that put that power on consumer graphics cards. The core question is practical: pixel diffusion makes beautiful images but costs enormous compute, so where can the work be done more cheaply without losing quality? You should care because this is the step that turned diffusion from a laboratory result into a tool artists could actually download and run.
  • Step 2 — The words you need: Latent space is a compressed map that keeps an image’s meaning with far fewer numbers — a 256-pixel image at factor 8 becomes a 32×32 map. Perceptual compression throws away details humans barely see while preserving what looks real, judged by deep-feature and adversarial losses rather than raw pixel differences. Semantic compression is what remains: objects, layouts, styles, the diffusion model’s job. An autoencoder squeezes an image to latent and expands it back. Cross-attention lets image features query text features. Classifier-free guidance pushes samples toward the prompt at sampling time. FID scores realism and diversity; lower is better.
  • Step 3 — How it worked before: Every generator had a painful trade. GANs were fast and sharp but unstable, often collapsing to a narrow range of images. Autoregressive transformers handled complex scenes but needed billions of parameters and generated pixel by pixel, extremely slowly. Diffusion, introduced by DDPM and relatives, beat GANs on quality, but it worked directly in pixel space: every training step and every sampling step processed the full-resolution red-green-blue grid. The paper reports that top pixel diffusion models needed 150 to 1000 V100 GPU-days to train.
  • Step 4 — Where it broke: The break point was access. Generating 50,000 samples could take about five days on a single A100, so only well-funded labs could train or even experiment, and each run left a large carbon footprint. Worse, most of that compute was spent on imperceptible high-frequency detail — fabric weave, film grain — which the diffusion model had to redraw at every denoising step. The waste was structural: pixel space forces the creative model to act as a pixel-perfect renderer, when its real value is composition and meaning.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — SQUEEZE, DREAM, DECODE: THE THREE-STAGE MACHINE

  • Step 5 — The central insight: Separate seeing details from imagining scenes. An architect first draws a rough floor plan on a napkin, then a painter renders every brick and window. The napkin holds the meaning; the painter adds the texture. Latent Diffusion does the same in two stages: a perceptual autoencoder compresses photos into small latent maps that still look right, and a diffusion model invents new latent maps from pure noise. Because the map is 4 to 16 times smaller in each spatial direction, the diffusion model juggles far fewer numbers, so it trains faster, samples faster, and spends its capacity on layout, objects, and style.
  • Step 6 — Stage one: perceptual compression: An encoder turns a height×width×3 image into a smaller×smaller×few-channels latent tensor; downsampling factor f means factor 4 turns a 256×256 photo into a 64×64 map. A decoder reconstructs the photo. Training combines three losses: reconstruction error, a perceptual loss comparing deep image features so results look natural rather than blurry, and a patch-based adversarial loss where a discriminator judges small patches. Two gentle regularizers are tested — a tiny KL penalty toward a standard normal, or vector quantization snapping values to a learned codebook — and compression is deliberately kept mild.
  • Step 7 — Stage two: the denoising objective, decoded: Every training image is encoded once into a latent map; forward diffusion adds Gaussian noise over about 1000 steps until it is static. A time-conditional U-Net of 2D convolutions with attention at selected resolutions learns to reverse this. Decoded: encode a random image, pick a random noise level, add noise, and ask the network to predict exactly what was added. The loss is L = E[‖ε − ε_θ(z_t, t, c)‖²] — the expected squared distance between true noise ε and the prediction ε_θ, where z_t is the noisy latent, t the step, and c the conditioning. The network never touches full-resolution pixels in its inner loop.
  • Step 8 — Stage three: conditioning and inference: Spatial controls — a low-resolution image for super-resolution, a masked image for inpainting, a semantic map — are downsampled and concatenated channel-wise to the noisy latent. General conditions like text prompts go through cross-attention: a domain-specific encoder turns the prompt into embeddings, and each U-Net layer attends to it, with queries from image features and keys and values from text. Encoder and U-Net train jointly with the same noise loss. At inference, start from random latent noise, run 50 to 250 DDIM steps with optional classifier-free guidance around scale 1.5, then decode once. Convolutions also allow tiling to about 1024 pixels and beyond.
  • Step 9 — One prompt, walked through: Take the prompt a red hat on a snowy street at 512×512. Encoded at factor 8, the image’s 786,432 pixel values become a 64×64×4 latent of only 16,384 numbers — about 48 times fewer. Sampling begins with pure Gaussian noise of that shape and runs 50 DDIM steps; at each step the U-Net predicts noise, conditioned through cross-attention on the prompt’s CLIP embeddings, so the words steer denoising toward a hat and snow. After the final step, one pass through the decoder expands the finished latent into the full photograph. The expensive loop ran small; only the last step paid full resolution.

STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — THE SPEED-QUALITY PROOF, THE CAVEATS, AND STABLE DIFFUSION

  • Step 10 — What the numbers proved: Smaller latents won on both axes. On ImageNet, training for two million steps on one A100, the factor-8 latent model beat pixel diffusion by about 38 FID points and sampled several times faster. Unconditional records fell: FID 5.11 on CelebA-HQ, 4.98 on FFHQ, 4.02 on LSUN-Churches, and 2.95 on LSUN-Bedrooms — the last close to the far larger pixel model ADM while using about half the parameters and a quarter of the compute. On ImageNet 256, the 400-million-parameter factor-4 model hit 10.56 FID unguided and 3.60 with guidance at scale 1.5, beating ADM with guidance at 4.59, with Inception score around 247.
  • Step 11 — Why the design works: Most pixel values in a photo carry no perceptible information; the autoencoder absorbs that high-frequency detail once, and the diffusion model spends its capacity on semantics. Shrinking the spatial grid also multiplies the payoff of every denoising step, so many more sampling steps fit in the same budget — and those extra steps are what perceptual quality needs. Cross-attention binds words to regions precisely: text embeddings become keys and values, image features become queries, so a red hat can influence exactly the pixels it should. And because the U-Net is fully convolutional, one model tiles to resolutions it never trained on.
  • Step 12 — Honest limits: The authors are candid that latent diffusion still trails GANs on speed because sampling remains sequential over many steps. The autoencoder is a ceiling for pixel-perfect work: tiny text, faces, and scientific imagery can never be fixed once the encoder has thrown detail away, and super-resolution models inherit that limit. Easing image creation also eases deepfakes, misinformation, and non-consensual imagery, can leak training data, and may amplify dataset biases, since an adversarial first stage plus a likelihood-based second stage can still misrepresent underrepresented groups.
  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: This paper is the direct bridge from DDPM (05) to the world: it uses the same denoising objective and DDIM sampling, and its famous text-to-image version conditions on frozen CLIP (06) ViT-L/14 embeddings. By proving that factor-4 to factor-8 latents preserve detail at a fraction of the cost, it gave the exact recipe for Stable Diffusion v1, which runs in under 10 GB of video memory. The open release ignited a community of fine-tunes, ControlNet, and LoRA (11) adapters, and its prompt-steering spirit parallels InstructGPT (08), LLaMA 2 (13), DPO (14), and DeepSeek-R1 (15).

InstructGPT — puppy training in 3 gears (SFT→RM→PPO)

1 · PROBLEM — WEB PARROT, NOT ASSISTANT

GPT-3 could continue a user’s question by writing more questions, ignore a request such as “answer in two paragraphs,” and hallucinate on 41% of closed-domain questions; it could also become toxic or biased when prompted. Few-shot prompting helped little, while instruction-tuned FLAN and T0 focused on classification and question answering rather than the open-ended brainstorming and generation customers used through the API. The challenge was to teach the model the nuanced goals of being helpful, honest, and harmless without repeating its enormous pretraining run.

2 · HOW — 3 STEPS

  1. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT): GPT-3 models with 1.3, 6, and 175 billion parameters and a 2,000-token context are fine-tuned on about 13,000 demonstrations written by API users and labelers. They train for 16 epochs, and checkpoints are selected by reward rather than validation loss.
  2. Reward model (RM): For about 33,000 prompts, labelers rank four to nine candidate responses. A 6-billion-parameter SFT model with a scalar output head learns these pairwise preferences; training uses one batch element per prompt and a zero-centred reward.
  3. Proximal policy optimization (PPO): The model practices on unlabeled prompts in a bandit-style loop. Its reward is the frozen RM score minus β times the per-token Kullback–Leibler (KL) distance, with β=0.02, plus a pretraining-data mix weighted by γ=27.8 (PTX). Training uses 256,000 episodes and batches of 512. At inference, the resulting model is sampled normally.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

Human rankings are distilled into an automatic judge that can score many responses, and reinforcement learning uses those scores to improve the assistant. The KL penalty keeps the updated policy near its supervised starting point, while mixing in pretraining examples helps preserve general language ability and reduce the cost of alignment. The three stages work together: demonstrate a good response, learn which responses people prefer, and practice improving without drifting too far from the original model.

r = rφ(x,y) − β·KL(π||πSFT) + γ·logPpretrain
In plain terms, the reward combines the judge’s score with a penalty for drifting from the starting model and a bonus for retaining pretraining knowledge.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE THE PAPER: FLUENT GIANTS WITH NO MANNERS

  • Step 1 — What alignment means: Alignment is training a model to act in line with what users actually intend, summarized as helpful, honest, and harmless. Pretraining only teaches a machine to continue text fluently; alignment turns it into something that answers your question instead of continuing the internet. This paper is the founding document of the chatbot era, so understanding it explains why today’s assistants are usable at all.
  • Step 2 — The vocabulary of the three gears: Fine-tuning means continuing training on new examples by updating weights with gradients. SFT, supervised fine-tuning, is simply imitating human-written ideal answers. A reward model is a separate network that reads a prompt plus answer and emits one quality score mimicking human preference. PPO, proximal policy optimization, improves the policy in small clipped steps, and a KL penalty charges the model for drifting from its supervised starting point.
  • Step 3 — How the world worked before 2022: GPT-3 (03) was a decoder trained to predict the next internet word, steered at usage time by few-shot prompts. Prompt tricks helped a little but never guaranteed obedience. Public instruction sets like FLAN and T0 offered fine-tuning data, yet they covered mostly classification and question answering, while real API traffic was open-ended generation and brainstorming.

The field needed to inject fuzzy human values without rewriting the whole internet training set.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — THREE GEARS: DEMONSTRATE, RANK, THEN REWARD

  • Step 5 — The insight: preference is a teachable score: Treat human preference as a teachable score, like training a puppy: demonstrate the trick, give treats for better attempts, withhold them for worse ones. InstructGPT runs three gears — humans demonstrate ideal answers, humans rank model answers to train an automatic judge, then reinforcement learning nudges the model toward high-reward answers while a leash keeps it close to its old behavior.
  • Step 6 — Gear one: SFT warm-up: All three model sizes start from GPT-3 decoders at 1.3B, 6B, and 175B parameters. About 13,000 prompts came from the API Playground and labelers, and humans wrote ideal demonstrations to imitate for 16 epochs with cosine decay and dropout.   • Step 7 — Gear two: the reward-model judge: For ≈33,000 prompts the SFT model and variants generated 4 to 9 answers each, and labelers ranked them best to worst. A 6B model, initialized from SFT with its word-prediction layer swapped for a single-number score head, learns one rule in plain English: for every pair where answer A beat B, push the score gap wider so the judge assigns high probability to the human choice. All pairs from one prompt share a single batch element to avoid correlated overfitting.
  • Step 8 — Gear three: PPO with a KL leash: The SFT policy now plays a bandit game: it receives a random customer prompt, writes a response, and earns the frozen reward model’s score minus a small per-token KL penalty for drifting from SFT — that penalty blocks reward hacking. At inference nothing extra runs — you simply prompt the final model.
  • Step 9 — One prompt through all three gears: Take Explain photosynthesis in two sentences, no jargon. During SFT a labeler writes exactly such an answer; later the system generates 4 to 9 candidate answers for that prompt and humans rank them; the reward model learns that the crisp two-sentence version outscores a rambling one. Under PPO the model samples a reply, collects that score minus KL drift, and nudges weights upward — so at the end, your same prompt returns a crisp two-sentence answer from ordinary sampling.

Chain-of-Thought — 8 examples teach giants to think aloud

1 · PROBLEM — JUMP-AND-SLIP

Ordinary few-shot prompts that show a question and its answer could retrieve familiar facts, but performance stalled on multi-step mathematics and commonsense problems. The model often jumped directly to an answer and made an arithmetic or logical mistake along the way. The usual fixes were to fine-tune separately on thousands of written rationales, as in Cobbe’s verifier work, or to build brittle neuro-symbolic solvers. Both approaches were expensive, specialized, and difficult to reuse across tasks at serving time.

2 · HOW — 0 TRAINING, ONLY PROMPT

  1. Keep the models frozen: The experiments use LaMDA-137B, GPT-3 and davinci-002, PaLM at 8, 62, and 540 billion parameters, UL2, and Codex. None of their weights are updated.
  2. Provide worked examples: A prompt includes eight GSM8K examples that show a question, reasoning sentences, and a final phrase such as “the answer is X”; one example reasons that Roger has 5+6=11 tennis balls. Other experiments use four AQuA examples and reasoning traces for CommonsenseQA (CSQA), StrategyQA, last-letter tasks, and coin-flip tasks.
  3. Generate the reasoning: The test question is appended to the examples and the model generates greedily. Next-token prediction continues a written scratchpad before producing the answer, breaking a hard problem into steps, spending additional compute tokens, and exposing the generated trace for inspection.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

A written English scratchpad turns one difficult jump into smaller steps that can be checked, and generating those extra tokens gives the model more computation for the problem. The paper finds this latent reasoning behaviour appears most clearly once a model is large enough; smaller networks often lack the capacity to carry out the same procedure. Because the examples demonstrate the reasoning format, the model can continue that pattern at test time without fine-tuning.

prompt = [Q₁+R₁+A₁ … Q₈+R₈+A₈] + Qtest → Rtest + A
In plain terms, the model sees eight examples with questions, reasoning, and answers, then continues the same format for the new question.
◍ The authors changed the prompts rather than fine-tuning any model, which surprised reviewers accustomed to training runs. Sundar Pichai demonstrated chain-of-thought prompting at Google I/O 2022, and the paper’s eight handwritten math examples inspired follow-up methods such as self-consistency and least-to-most prompting.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE THE PAPER: ANSWERS WITH NO WORKING SHOWN

  • Step 1 — What reasoning means for a token predictor: A language model answers by predicting one token at a time, so every multi-step deduction must somehow survive in its output. A chain of thought is a series of intermediate natural-language steps that lead to a final answer, like showing your working in a math exam. This paper is the reason modern models think step by step before answering.
  • Step 2 — The vocabulary you need first: Few-shot prompting means putting a handful of input-output examples inside the prompt so the model learns the pattern with no weight updates; standard prompting shows only question and answer. GSM8K is a benchmark of ≈1,319 grade-school word problems needing two to eight steps. An emergent ability is a skill absent in small models that appears suddenly past a scale threshold — here roughly 100 billion parameters.
  • Step 3 — How people tried to get reasoning before: Following GPT-3 (03), everyone prompted with bare question-then-answer pairs, which handles recall fine. The expensive alternative was hiring experts to write thousands of rationales and fine-tuning a separate model per task, as in Cobbe and colleagues’ verifier work, or building neuro-symbolic systems that output formal equations. Both cost heavily, needed special training, and stayed brittle across tasks.
  • Step 4 — Where it broke, concretely: On GSM8K, accuracy stayed low and flat as models grew, because the model jumped straight to the final number and made arithmetic or logic slips on the way. Even a two-hop problem like counting balls across cans was enough to fail. The field needed multi-step reasoning from one frozen checkpoint serving many tasks, with no fine-tuning budget at all.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — SCRATCH PAPER IN PLAIN ENGLISH, BUILT LINE BY LINE

  • Step 5 — The idea: make the model show its work: Imagine two math teachers — one accepts only final numbers, one requires each line of working. The second class catches its own errors. Chain-of-thought prompting prompts with question, a short natural-language derivation, then the answer; at test time the model imitates that style and writes its own intermediate sentences. That scratch paper spreads computation across more tokens and makes the path checkable, in plain English that transfers across tasks.
  • Step 6 — Crucially, nothing is trained: There is no new architecture and no training — that is the point. The authors take off-the-shelf autoregressive transformers: LaMDA up to 137B, GPT-3 variants up to 175B including text-davinci-002, PaLM at 8B, 62B, and 540B, plus UL2 20B and Codex. Only the prompt text changes, proving a frozen checkpoint can rival task-specific fine-tuning — which made prompting the default interface for complex work.
  • Step 7 — How the exemplars are written: For arithmetic the team hand-wrote eight exemplars, each a question, a few sentences of reasoning, and a final line like the answer is 11: Roger starts with 5 balls, 2 cans of 3 is 6 more, so 5 plus 6 is 11. AQuA algebra used four. Commonsense sets like CSQA and StrategyQA got chains that eliminate options or state implicit facts — a pear’s density ≈ 0.6 g/cm³ makes it float. Symbolic tasks name each last letter before joining.
  • Step 8 — What happens at test time: The test question is appended after the frozen exemplars and the model generates greedily until it stops. It is still next-token prediction, but the prompt established think-then-answer, so a coherent chain is the most likely continuation. The paper gives three reasons it works: decomposition into easier steps, more tokens of compute for harder problems, and an interpretable window where humans see reasoning break. Ablations show the steps must precede the answer and be meaningful language, not dots.
  • Step 9 — One problem, walked end to end: Take Roger has 5 tennis balls and buys 2 cans of 3. The model first writes Roger starts with 5 balls — one clause per fact — then 2 cans of 3 is 6 more, then the arithmetic 5 plus 6 is 11, then the answer is 11. Each token of working is another forward pass, so a three-hop problem gets three hops’ worth of computation instead of one jump to a guess.

STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — REASONING AS AN EMERGENT SKILL — PROOF, LIMITS, LEGACY

  • Step 10 — What the numbers showed: On GSM8K, PaLM 540B rose from ≈17.9 percent with standard prompting to 56.9 with chain-of-thought and 58.6 with a calculator, beating a fine-tuned GPT-3 with verifier at ≈55. GPT-3 175B jumped 15.6 → 46.9 and Codex 19.7 → 63.1. Commonsense rose too: StrategyQA 68.6 → 77.8, sports understanding 80.5 → 95.4 — above an unaided fan’s 84. Symbolic tasks length-generalized: 99.4 percent on two-word names, ≈63 percent on four-word names never shown.
  • Step 11 — Why the mechanism creates reasoning: The chain externalizes intermediate state that one forward pass cannot hold, turning depth-limited computation into token-limited computation. Gains grow with scale and difficulty — easy one-step MAWPS items barely moved — which is why this counts as an emergent ability past ≈100B parameters. Ablations placing equations alone, dots alone, or reasoning after the answer all fell back near baseline: the thinking must be real language, and it must come before the answer.
  • Step 12 — Honest limits: Only large models benefit: below about 10B parameters the chains are fluent but illogical and performance drops. Even at 540B, among wrong GSM8K answers ≈46 percent were nearly right with a calculator slip while 54 percent had major coherence errors, and correct answers can come from bad logic. Long chains cost tokens and latency, factuality is not guaranteed, and serving a 500B reasoner is expensive for real products.
  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: This paper is the conceptual parent of DeepSeek-R1 (15), which turns manual chains into learned long reasoning with reinforcement learning and self-consistency. It is routinely combined with LoRA (11) for cheap reasoning adapters and FlashAttention (12) for long reasoning contexts, while LLaMA 2 (13) chat models are evaluated with chain-of-thought prompts. It built on GPT-3 (03) few-shot learning and was tested on InstructGPT (08) variants, sharpening the question Chinchilla (10) asks: how much compute does reasoning deserve?
2023 · Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model · Rafailov et al., Stanford · NeurIPS 2023 Read Paper 14 notes

DPO — your LM is secretly a reward model (10 lines)

1 · PROBLEM — PPO PAIN

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) with proximal policy optimization (PPO) needed four networks: the policy being trained, a frozen reference policy, a reward model, and a value model. It also required online response sampling and careful tuning of the Kullback–Leibler penalty, clipping, advantages, and reward normalization. High-variance updates could exploit the reward judge with gibberish, collapse into one safe-sounding response, or exploit a frozen reward model on inputs unlike its training data. The complexity and compounded errors across stages made this alignment approach difficult for smaller research teams to run.

2 · HOW — 3 STEPS

  1. Reference model and data: The method freezes a supervised fine-tuned reference policy πref (or uses Preferred-FT, trained only on chosen responses). Training data is a static set of triples (x,yw,yl) containing a prompt, a preferred answer, and a rejected answer, drawn from TL;DR, HH-170k, and synthetic IMDb preferences. No new responses are sampled during training.
  2. Derivation: The optimum under a KL constraint is π*=πref·exp(r/β)/Z, which rearranges to r=βlog(π*/πref)+βlogZ. Substituting this expression into the Bradley–Terry preference model P(w>l)=σ(rw−rl) cancels the normalization constant Z, expressing preferences directly in terms of policy probabilities.
  3. Loss and training: The policy minimizes −logσ(β[logπ(w)/ref(w) − logπ(l)/ref(l)]) using summed token log-probabilities. Training uses RMSprop with a learning rate of 1e-6, batches of 64, and one to three epochs; responses are generated normally after training.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) derives a simpler alignment loss directly from preference pairs. The policy’s log-probability ratio against its reference acts as an implicit reward, so there is no separate reward network to train. The comparison loss raises the probability of the preferred answer and lowers the rejected answer, with the update size depending on how badly the current model ranks the pair: an already-correct comparison receives a small change, while a reversed preference receives a larger one. This avoids the repetitive output collapse associated with simpler unlikelihood-style training.

r̂ = β·log πθ/πref · L=−logσ(r̂w−r̂l)
In plain terms, compare how much the tuned policy prefers the winner and the loser relative to the reference, then widen that preference gap.
◍ Rafael Rafailov, Archit Sharma, and Eric Mitchell contributed equally and are listed junior-first in the author list. The official implementation fits DPO in about ten lines of code, and follow-up methods include IPO, KTO, ORPO, and SimPO.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE THE PAPER: RLHF TOOK FOUR MODELS AND NERVES

  • Step 1 — What preference learning is for: After pretraining, a language model imitates the average internet — manners included and excluded. Alignment steers it toward helpful, honest, harmless behavior, and preference learning does that by showing the model which of two answers a human liked better. DPO is the trick that makes this as simple as ordinary supervised training, and it explains how most open chat models are tuned today.
  • Step 2 — Vocabulary before the algebra: RLHF means reinforcement learning from human feedback: human judgments become a reward signal. PPO is the stable RL algorithm that improves a policy in small clipped steps using a value model. The reference policy is the frozen supervised model anchoring training, with beta controlling the KL leash that punishes drifting from it. Bradley-Terry is the standard pairwise-choice rule: the chance of picking one answer is a softmax over hidden quality scores.
  • Step 3 — How alignment worked in InstructGPT: InstructGPT (08) defined the heavy three-stage recipe: supervised fine-tuning, a separate reward model trained on ranked answers, then PPO. Training held four large networks at once — the policy, a frozen copy of it, the reward model, and a value model — sampled fresh generations every step, and updated with high-variance policy gradients, reward normalization, advantage normalization, and clipping.
  • Step 4 — Where that machinery broke: Small mistakes in KL strength or learning rate caused reward hacking, where the model spouts gibberish that fools the judge, or mode collapse into one safe answer. Worse, the reward model is frozen while PPO explores answers it never saw, so judge errors compound. Only well-funded labs could afford the memory and the tuning sweeps; everyone else wanted alignment quality with supervised-learning simplicity.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — THE REWARD MODEL HIDING INSIDE THE POLICY

  • Step 5 — The insight: your LM is secretly a reward model: Picture two exam answers where the teacher prefers A. Traditional RLHF hires a separate judge; DPO notices the student already carries an implicit judge — how much more the current model likes A than a well-behaved reference does, measured as a log-ratio of probabilities. Training simply widens that gap: push the winner up, push the loser down, weighted by how wrong you currently are. The beta knob is the leash to the reference.
  • Step 6 — Static data and a reference, prepared once: Take the supervised fine-tuned model as the reference; if none exists, first fine-tune the base model only on chosen answers to get a Preferred-FT reference. Then collect static triples of prompt, winner, and loser: the reference samples two answers and a human picks the better one. The paper reuses Reddit TL;DR summaries, Anthropic HH dialogues with ≈170 thousand pairs, and IMDb prefixes with synthetic sentiment preferences — no online sampling is needed later.
  • Step 7 — The algebra that deletes the reward network: Standard RLHF maximizes expected reward minus beta × KL from the reference. That constrained problem has a closed-form optimum: the best policy equals the reference × exp(reward ÷ beta), normalized by a partition term Z summing over all answers. Take logs and rearrange: reward = beta × log(policy ÷ reference) + beta × log Z. In words, reward is just how much more the tuned policy likes an answer than the reference does, plus a prompt-specific constant.
  • Step 8 — The loss, decoded: Human choice follows Bradley-Terry, a softmax over scores; substituting the implicit reward cancels Z because it is identical for both answers. The DPO loss is negative log-sigmoid of beta × (log-ratio of winner minus log-ratio of loser), from summed token log-probabilities in two passes — policy and frozen reference. The gradient pushes the winner up and loser down, weighted by current error — unlike unlikelihood training, which collapses into repeating when when when. Settings are gentle: beta 0.1, RMSprop 1e-6, one to three epochs.
  • Step 9 — One preference pair, walked through: Take Summarize this Reddit post. The reference samples summaries A and B; the labeler prefers A. Both models score both texts — say the policy gives A log-prob -12.0, B -11.5, the reference A -12.5, B -11.8. The log-ratios are +0.5 for A and +0.3 for B; × beta 0.1 their gap is +0.02, sigmoid ≈ 0.505. The model ranks the pair barely right, so the gradient nudges A up and B down — confidently ranked pairs shrink to almost no update.

STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — SIMPLER THAN PPO, BETTER THAN PPO, STILL FLAWED

  • Step 10 — What the experiments proved: On IMDb, where a ground-truth sentiment classifier acts as perfect judge, DPO’s reward-versus-KL frontier sits strictly above PPO — even PPO given the true reward. On Reddit TL;DR, GPT-4 and humans preferred DPO over human reference summaries ≈61 percent, versus PPO’s 57 and supervised tuning’s 30 to 40; humans picked DPO over PPO ≈58 percent. On Anthropic HH, DPO was the only offline method beating the human-chosen answers, matching best-of-128 sampling without search. It generalized better: 36 vs 26 percent on CNN DailyMail.
  • Step 11 — Why the shortcut is exact, not approximate: The KL-constrained RLHF objective already has a closed-form solution, so the reward is redundant — it is readable from how the policy reweights the reference. Because the loss remains a likelihood on static data, training is as stable as supervised learning, and the error-weighted gradient prevents both runaway down-weighting of losers and the mode collapse PPO suffers when the judge is imperfect. Z cancelling is why no reward network is needed at all.
  • Step 12 — Honest limits: DPO is offline: it never sees fresh generations, so overtraining for hundreds of epochs leaks probability mass into unseen gibberish and performance collapses. The gradient lowers the loser faster than it raises the winner, so fluency can sag; summed token log-probs also bias toward verbosity — outputs run about twice as long as the labels. Bradley-Terry assumes clean transitive preferences, a good reference is required, and the paper only tested up to 6B models judged by GPT-4.
  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: DPO turned alignment into a recipe any small lab could run: it is the practical companion to LLaMA 2-Chat (13), and paired with LoRA (11) and FlashAttention (12) it made 7B alignment feasible on a single GPU. DeepSeek-R1 (15) answers DPO’s offline limits by returning to online RL with verifiable rule rewards and group advantages. The lineage runs through InstructGPT (08) and GPT-3 (03) back to the Transformer (01), while Chinchilla (10) reminds us preference-data quality beats quantity.
2021 · LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models · Hu et al., Microsoft · ICLR 2022 Read Paper 11 notes

LoRA — freeze the tower, train sticky notes (r=1–8)

1 · PROBLEM — 35TB FOR 100 TASKS

Fully fine-tuning GPT-3’s 175 billion parameters created a checkpoint of about 350 gigabytes for each task, while the Adam optimizer required about 1.2 terabytes of graphics-card memory (VRAM). Keeping versions for 100 tasks would therefore take roughly 35 terabytes before accounting for optimizer-state swapping. Existing adapters added 20–30% latency at batch size one, and prefix-tuning both consumed context-window space and trained unstably. The goal was to switch tasks without multiplying storage, slowing inference, or maintaining separate serving systems.

2 · HOW — 3 STEPS

  1. Learn a low-rank update: Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) keeps the pretrained matrix W₀(d×k) frozen, while representing the update ΔW as B(d×r)A(r×k), with rank r=1–8 compared with d=12,288. The forward computation is h=W₀x+(α/r)BAx. Matrix A is initialized from a normal distribution and B starts at zero, so the initial model exactly matches the pretrained model.
  2. Train only the adapters: The ordinary next-token objective maximizes ΣlogP(w|ctx; W₀+BA), but only A and B are optimized. They are attached to the attention matrices Wq, Wk, Wv, and Wo, while the multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) and all other pretrained weights remain frozen.
  3. Deploy: The update can be merged into the original weights as W=W₀+BA, adding zero inference latency. Task-specific adapters of about 35 megabytes can instead be swapped in or out, and token decoding otherwise stays unchanged.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

Task adaptation appears to have a low intrinsic dimension: useful changes can be expressed through a small number of directions rather than rewriting every model weight. LoRA amplifies quieter pretrained directions, measuring energy of 6.91 compared with 0.32 for the projection of dominant directions, or roughly 21 times as much. A small learned subspace is therefore sufficient for many tasks, and merging its update into the original matrix removes any adapter overhead during inference.

h = W₀x + (α/r)·B·A·x · W ← W₀+BA
In plain terms, a small trainable update is added to the frozen model and can be merged into its weights before serving.
◍ Edward Hu said his Microsoft team began from a practical question: whether GPT-3 could work in products such as natural-language-to-code. A full GPT-3 checkpoint was about one terabyte when optimizer states were included, so the team designed a compact update that the community later joked could fit in an email attachment.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE THE PAPER: EVERY TASK NEEDED A NEW GIANT

  • Step 1 — Why anyone needs to customize a giant: Companies rarely want the generic model — they want it writing their code, speaking in their style, summarizing their documents. That means fine-tuning: continuing training on new examples by updating the weights with gradients. LoRA is the standard way to do this without a supercomputer, and it is why today you can download a tiny adapter file instead of a whole model.
  • Step 2 — Vocabulary for the tower: Full fine-tuning updates every parameter for the new task. Rank r is the inner size of the two small matrices LoRA learns: rank 1 means the update is a single pattern, rank 8 means eight. Intrinsic dimension says the useful change for a task lives in a tiny landscape despite billions of knobs. A checkpoint is the saved trained numbers, and inference latency is the extra waiting time when answering.
  • Step 3 — The pre-LoRA world, concretely: Before LoRA, customization meant updating all 175 billion parameters of GPT-3 (03) per task — ≈350 gigabytes to store one fine-tuned copy and ≈1.2 terabytes of GPU memory to train with Adam. Adapter layers inserted extra blocks run sequentially; prefix-tuning reserved input space for trainable virtual words. The pretrain-then-fine-tune paradigm itself came from the Transformer (01) attention blocks and BERT (02).
  • Step 4 — Where it broke, with the customer example: One hundred customers meant ≈35 terabytes of checkpoints and slow model switching. Adapters added 20.7 to 30.3 percent extra delay at batch size one — exactly how chat services run. Prefix-tuning was hard to optimize and stole space from the real question. Fine-tuning clearly beat few-shot prompting for real products, yet nobody could afford it; the field needed something tiny to store, fast to train, and free at inference.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — FREEZE THE TOWER, TRAIN THE STICKY NOTES

  • Step 5 — The insight: the useful change is low-rank: The update a new task needs lives in a very small subspace. Instead of learning a giant change matrix, LoRA learns two skinny matrices B with shape d × r and A with shape r × k, whose product BA is the change. Think of a huge office building: full fine-tuning rebuilds every wall, adapters add hallways everyone must walk through, while LoRA leaves the building intact and hands each tenant sticky notes — peel off, stick on, done.
  • Step 6 — The forward pass, symbol by symbol: A frozen layer computes h = W₀x, with x the incoming signal and h the outgoing one. LoRA computes h = W₀x + BAx. A starts as small Gaussian noise and B starts as zeros, so BA begins as exactly zero and the model initially behaves exactly like the original. Only A and B receive gradients; BAx is scaled by alpha ÷ r to keep learning stable across ranks, and in practice alpha is set to the first r tried.
  • Step 7 — Training objective and free deployment: The loss is the ordinary language-model objective: maximize the summed log-probability of each correct next word given the question and the previous words, where the probability comes from the frozen model plus the tiny update — reward the sticky notes whenever they help guess the next word. Because the design is pure addition, deployment merges W = W₀ + BA once, giving zero extra latency; switching tasks means subtracting one BA and adding the next.
  • Step 8 — Where the sticky notes go: The authors applied LoRA mainly to the attention matrices Wq, Wk, Wv, and Wo — the query, key, value, and output projections of Transformer (01) attention — and froze the MLP blocks. With a budget of 18 million trainable parameters on GPT-3, adapting Wq and Wv together worked best; putting everything into only Wq or only Wk was clearly worse. Touching two kinds of attention weights lightly beats touching one heavily.
  • Step 9 — One task through the machine: Take the E2E restaurant task: input like name = The Eagle, eatType = coffee shop enters a frozen attention layer computing h = W₀x + BAx — the original plus the rank-4 sticky note. At rank 4 the skinny matrices hold under a hundred thousand numbers per adapted weight, and the GPT-3 run trains just 4.7 million numbers against 175 billion frozen. The description generates as in full fine-tuning, and the GPT-3 adapter on disk is ≈35 megabytes instead of 350 gigabytes.

STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — THE TABLES, THE CAVEATS, AND THE ADOPTION WAVE

  • Step 10 — The proof: smaller and better: LoRA matched or beat full fine-tuning everywhere. RoBERTa-base with 0.3M trainable parameters averaged 87.2 on GLUE versus 86.4 for full fine-tuning; DeBERTa-XXL with 4.7M hit 91.3 versus 91.1; GPT-2 on E2E scored BLEU 70.4 versus 68.2. On GPT-3 175B, 4.7M parameters matched full fine-tuning on WikiSQL (73.4 percent) and MNLI (91.7). Efficiency: 10,000× fewer trainable parameters, GPU memory 1.2 TB → 350 GB, checkpoint 350 GB → ≈35 MB, training 25 percent faster.
  • Step 11 — Why rank 1 to 8 is enough: The subspace study explains it: the top singular direction of a rank-8 A matrix overlaps strongly with the rank-64 one (Grassmann similarity above 0.5), the rest looks like noise, and the same top direction appears across random seeds. The update also amplifies quiet patterns — ΔW’s energy is ≈21× the projection of W onto it (6.91 versus 0.32). LoRA boosts what pretraining ignored, so rank 1 already scores 73.4 on WikiSQL.
  • Step 12 — Honest limits: Tiny ranks can fail when the task is a new language or a far domain. The authors adapted only attention weights, leaving MLP blocks, layer norms, and biases untested. Merged weights make multi-task batching awkward — each example would need its own W — and keeping adapters separate costs some speed. Choosing which matrices to adapt and which rank to use is still trial and error, not theory.
  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: LoRA made alignment affordable: the expensive human-feedback tuning of InstructGPT (08) became single-GPU work, LLaMA 2 (13) chat models are almost always aligned with LoRA or QLoRA, DPO (14) tutorials use LoRA by default, and DeepSeek-R1 (15)-style reasoning distillation ships tiny adapters instead of whole models. It spawned QLoRA, DoRA, and the HuggingFace PEFT library, plus downloadable adapters for Latent Diffusion (07) models.
2022 · FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness · Dao et al., Stanford · NeurIPS 2022 Read Paper 12 notes

FlashAttention — exact, IO-aware: walk less (SRAM vs HBM)

1 · PROBLEM — MEMORY-BOUND, NOT MATH-BOUND

Standard attention materializes the score matrix S=QKT and the probability matrix P=softmax(S), both with N×N values; even a sequence length of N=1,024 creates more than one million values per head. Repeatedly writing and reading these matrices from high-bandwidth memory (HBM) for softmax, dropout, and backpropagation dominates runtime. Sparse and low-rank approximations reduce floating-point operations (FLOPs), but do not necessarily improve wall-clock time and can reduce quality. The challenge was to preserve exact attention while supporting long sequences without being overwhelmed by input/output (I/O) data movement.

2 · HOW — 3 STEPS

  1. Online tiled softmax: For each row, the algorithm tracks the running maximum m and exponential sum l=Σexp. When a new tile arrives, it updates mnew=max(m1,m2) and rescales the partial sums using lnew=l₁em₁−m+l₂em₂−m. This computes the exact softmax without storing a complete row.
  2. Forward pass: Query, key, and value tiles are chosen with Bc=M/4d so they fit in on-chip static random-access memory (SRAM), about 20 megabytes with bandwidth near 19 terabytes per second, rather than repeatedly accessing 40 gigabytes of HBM at about 1.5 terabytes per second. Nested loops compute τQKT, apply the mask, and update the output O and running l,m values on chip, yielding exact softmax(QKT)V.
  3. Backward pass: The kernel saves only O,l,m and random-number-generator (RNG) state, which requires O(Nd) memory. It recomputes P on chip and accumulates dV, dQ, and dK using D=dot(dO,O), combining the work in one fused kernel.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

On modern processors, fewer arithmetic operations do not always mean faster execution. FlashAttention deliberately recomputes some values, increasing work from 66.6 to 75.2 GFLOPs, to reduce memory traffic from 40.3 to 4.4 gigabytes and runtime from 41.7 to 7.3 milliseconds. Its tiling minimizes slow transfers while retaining the exact attention result, and its saved state grows linearly with sequence length rather than storing the full attention matrix.

O,l,m on-chip · P recomputed · save O(Nd)
In plain terms, the fast on-chip memory holds one tile of work, and the algorithm avoids repeatedly fetching the full attention matrix.
◍ FlashAttention can run faster while doing more arithmetic: in one GPT-2 medium case, work rose from 66.6 to 75.2 gigaFLOPs while runtime fell from 41.7 to 7.3 milliseconds as memory traffic dropped. Its Path-X result reached 61.4% on a 16,000-pixel maze task after earlier Transformers had reached chance or run out of memory.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE FLASHATTENTION: WHY THE GPU WAS WAITING ON MEMORY

  • Step 1 — What attention costs, and why you should care: Attention is how a Transformer (01) lets every word look at every earlier word before predicting the next one. With N words, standard attention builds an N × N score for every head, so a 1,024-word document already means more than a million numbers per head, and longer chats or books explode from there. If long-context AI feels expensive and slow, this memory traffic is the reason.
  • Step 2 — Six terms for the memory journey: HBM is the graphics card’s big, slow memory — about 40 GB at 1.5 TB/s on an A100. SRAM is tiny on-chip memory — about 20 MB at 19 TB/s, roughly twelve times faster. Tiling means chopping matrices into blocks that fit SRAM; recomputation means rebuilding big intermediates during the backward pass instead of storing them; IO-awareness means counting reads and writes, not just math; block-sparse attention skips whole blocks whose mask is zero.
  • Step 3 — How attention ran before the paper: Standard attention computes S = QKᵀ, then P = softmax(S) row by row, then O = PV, where Q, K, and V are the query, key, and value matrices of shape N × d. S and P are N × N, so the kernel writes two giant matrices to HBM and reads them back for masking, softmax, dropout, and again for gradients. Frameworks like PyTorch gave programmers no fine control over that traffic.
  • Step 4 — Exactly where it broke: On GPT-2 medium at length 1,024, forward attention did 66.6 gigaFLOPs but moved 40.3 GB to and from HBM and took 41.7 milliseconds — the math was never the bottleneck. Approximate methods like Linformer and Performer reduced arithmetic, yet wall-clock speed barely improved and quality dropped. Long tasks such as Path-X at 16,000 tokens simply ran out of memory or scored random chance.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — THE IDEA IN MOTION: TILE, RESCALE, RECOMPUTE

  • Step 5 — The insight: stop walking to the pantry: FlashAttention is IO-aware: it minimizes bytes moved between HBM and SRAM, not FLOPs. The analogy is cooking in a tiny kitchen with a pantry across the street. Standard attention carries every ingredient out, lays the whole feast on the sidewalk, then carries it back. FlashAttention brings one basket at a time into the kitchen, cooks that part completely, writes down only the recipe notes it needs, and repeats. The kitchen is SRAM; the pantry is HBM.
  • Step 6 — The computation, symbol by symbol: For each head, attention is S = τ · QKᵀ, where Q and K have shape N × d, τ = 1/√d is a temperature that keeps scores calm, and S holds how strongly each query matches each key. P = softmax(S) converts each row into weights that sum to one, and O = P · V mixes the value vectors by those weights. S and P are the N × N monsters; the trick is never to store them in HBM.
  • Step 7 — Online softmax: merging blocks without a whole row: Normal softmax finds the row maximum m, exponentiates each score shifted by m, sums them into l, then divides. Two blocks merge with three rules: the new maximum is the larger of the two; the new sum is each old sum reweighted by exp(old max − new max); the running output is the old output reweighted the same way plus the new block’s contribution, all divided by the new sum. So the row is built block by block.
  • Step 8 — The nested loops and the IO win: The kernel picks key/value block size Bc = M / (4d) and query block size Br = min(M / (4d), d), where M is SRAM. It loads each Kⱼ and Vⱼ block once, loops over query blocks inside, and updates O, m, and l in HBM. Backward saves only O, l, m, and the dropout seed, recomputing P on chip. Standard attention costs Θ(Nd + N²) HBM accesses; FlashAttention costs Θ(N²d²/M), up to 9 times fewer moves, and provably near-optimal.
  • Step 9 — Worked mini-example: one row, two key blocks: Take one query row with four keys split into two blocks. Block A scores [2, 0]: m = 2, exponentials [1, 0.135], l = 1.135. Block B scores [4, 1]: m = 4, exponentials [1, 0.050], l = 1.050. Merging gives new max 4 and new sum 1.135 × e⁻² + 1.050 ≈ 1.203, so block A keeps weight ≈ 0.127 and block B gets ≈ 0.873 — exactly the weights a full softmax over [2, 0, 4, 1] gives.

STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — THE EVIDENCE: FASTER, LONGER, AND NOW STANDARD

  • Step 10 — The numbers, and what they mean: BERT-large trained in 17.4 minutes versus 20.0 for MLPerf (15% faster) to 72% masked-language accuracy, and GPT-2 small in 2.7 days versus 9.5 for HuggingFace (3.5×) at the same perplexity 18.2. On GPT-2 medium, math rose from 66.6 to 75.2 gigaFLOPs while HBM traffic fell 40.3 → 4.4 GB and runtime 41.7 → 7.3 milliseconds. On Long-Range Arena it scored 59.8 versus 59.3 for standard attention while running 2.4× faster, and used 836 MB at 4,096 versus PyTorch’s ≈17 GB.
  • Step 11 — Why less walking beats more chopping: Modern GPUs compute far faster than they can feed memory, so kernels sat idle waiting for bytes; FLOPs no longer determine speed. FlashAttention accepts extra arithmetic, recomputing P and S during backward, because FLOPs are cheap and HBM moves are not. It is also exact, returning the same softmax answer with no quality drop, and runs up to 7.6× faster on attention itself. The optimality proof shows this is the right amount of motion for the problem, not a lucky hack.
  • Step 12 — Honest limits: Each new attention variant still needs hand-tuned CUDA, and kernels may not transfer cleanly across A100, H100, and consumer cards. The optimality proof covers a single GPU; splitting attention across many cards adds a communication layer the paper does not solve. Most importantly, FlashAttention reduces memory traffic and memory size, not the quadratic math — beyond tens of thousands of tokens you still need sparsity, linear attention, or state-space methods, which is why block-sparse attention is offered as a first step, not the finish line.
  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: FlashAttention became the invisible engine of the modern stack. LLaMA 2 (13) trained on two trillion tokens with it for longer contexts; DeepSeek-R1 (15) needs it to afford 65,000-token reasoning traces; Latent Diffusion (07) and CLIP (06) benefit in attention-heavy U-Nets and vision Transformers; InstructGPT (08) and DPO (14) sample candidate answers faster. It builds on the exact attention of the Transformer (01) and made BERT (02), GPT-3 (03), and RAG (04) long-context work practical, while sparking FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3 and native PyTorch support.

LLaMA 2 — open weights, closed-grade chat

1 · PROBLEM — TWO WORLDS

Closed assistants such as ChatGPT, Bard, and Claude were useful and safety-tuned through costly supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), but their models and training recipes were opaque and difficult to reproduce. Open models such as BLOOM, Falcon, and LLaMA 1 provided strong base weights, comparable to GPT-3 or Chinchilla, but were not trained to behave like assistants and could be unsafe in chat. The crucial alignment process remained hidden behind application programming interfaces (APIs), leaving researchers unable to study or afford the final step from a capable base model to a usable assistant.

2 · HOW — 3 STAGES

  1. Base model: Large Language Model Meta AI (LLaMA) 2 uses root-mean-square layer normalization (RMSNorm), SwiGLU feed-forward layers, and rotary positional embeddings (RoPE). The context window grows from 2,048 to 4,096 tokens. The 34-billion- and 70-billion-parameter models use grouped-query attention with eight key/value groups (GQA-8), which performs close to full multi-head attention and substantially better than a single group. Training uses a 32,000-token byte-pair encoding (BPE) vocabulary, two trillion tokens (40% more than LLaMA 1), an upsampled factual-data mix, a four-million-token batch, AdamW with cosine decay, and about 3.3 million A100 GPU-hours.
  2. Supervised fine-tuning: The assistant is trained on 27,540 carefully selected vendor demonstrations after discarding millions of lower-quality examples. Loss is applied to answers only, and training lasts two epochs, prioritizing example quality over quantity.
  3. RLHF, safety, and Ghost Attention: Training uses 1.4 million binary preference comparisons, or 2.9 million when the open comparisons are included, and separate helpfulness and safety reward models with ranking and margin losses. Across versions V1–V5, the team alternates rejection sampling—generating dozens of candidates and tuning on the best—with PPO and a KL penalty, then distills from the 70-billion-parameter model. Ghost Attention (GAtt) places the instruction into each turn during generation but trains on the first turn only; this retains the instruction at 20 turns with 100% success instead of roughly 0%.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

A strong pretrained base provides broad capability, while a small set of elite demonstrations teaches the assistant’s response style. Fresh on-policy comparisons and separate helpfulness and safety rewards let the system explore with rejection sampling and improve with PPO without forcing the two goals into one signal. Grouped-query attention keeps long conversations practical to serve, and publishing the training recipe makes the process reproducible for other teams.

SFT elite → dual-RM → reject+PPO → distill 70B→7/13B
In plain terms, the model learns from strong examples, compares helpfulness and safety, practices improving, and passes what it learns to smaller models.

  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: LLaMA 2 turned alignment from a closed secret into shared engineering. R1-Zero — bell at exit, no map (pure rule-RL, no SFT)

1 · PROBLEM — COPIED CHAINS DON’T VERIFY

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can copy human-written reasoning traces, but collecting those chains is expensive and imitation does not teach the model to check its own work. Proximal policy optimization (PPO) with a learned reward model can be exploited and needs a value network as large as the policy, which is difficult when later steps revise earlier reasoning. Chain-of-Thought prompting also relies on very large models—over 100 billion parameters—and many generated tokens. OpenAI’s o1 was strong but closed and expensive. The goal was verifiable reasoning that could be learned without human-written chains and later transferred to smaller models.

2 · HOW — ZERO CHAINS TAUGHT

  1. Prompt: The prompt is minimal: the user supplies a question, and the assistant is required to place its reasoning inside <think>…</think> tags before giving an answer. No reasoning content or worked chain is provided.
  2. Rule-based reward: Accuracy receives a score of one or zero. Mathematics is checked from a boxed answer with SymPy, code is checked with hidden tests, and multiple-choice questions require an exact match. A separate one-or-zero format reward checks the required tags. The method uses no neural reward model, and the two rewards have equal weight.
  3. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): For each question, the model samples 16 answers and normalizes each reward against the group mean and standard deviation, avoiding a separate value network. The KL coefficient is 0.001, the reference is synchronized every 400 steps, the learning rate is 3e-6, the sampling temperature is 1.0, and the batch size is 512. Training runs for 10,400 steps with context increasing from 32 to 65,000 tokens and 8,192 rollouts.

3 · WHY IT WORKS

The reward evaluates the final destination rather than prescribing every intermediate step, so exploration can discover that checking work and backtracking improve the chance of a correct answer; the model’s reasoning grows to about 20,000 tokens. GRPO compares sibling answers to the same question, which fits a reasoning process that may revise earlier steps better than a value network that must score partial thoughts. In this setup, the per-token KL penalty used in PPO could also discourage long reasoning simply because it contains more tokens.

Ai = (ri − mean(r)) / std(r) · J=clip-ratio·A − β·KL
In plain terms, the method raises the probability of answers that outperform their siblings, lowers it for worse answers, and lightly constrains the model to stay near its reference.

STEP BY STEP · PART 1/3 — BEFORE R1-ZERO: REASONING WAS IMITATED, NEVER INCENTIVIZED

  • Step 1 — The problem area: getting models to think in steps: Big chat models write fluently but stumble on multi-step math, code, and logic. Chain-of-Thought (09) showed that thinking aloud helps, yet the examples were handwritten and the benefit appeared only past roughly 100 billion parameters. R1-Zero asks whether reasoning can be grown from scratch instead of copied. This matters because reliable reasoning is what turns a fluent model into a tool you can hand a hard problem.
  • Step 2 — The vocabulary of learning by reward: Reinforcement learning means improving by trial and error from a reward signal, not by imitating examples. A rule reward is a checker that returns 1 or 0 — correct boxed math, passing code tests, clean format — with no learned judge to fool. GRPO, group relative policy optimization, compares answers to the same question and pushes better-than-average ones up. A value network is the separate critic that older PPO needs, and cold-start means a small supervised warm-up before RL.
  • Step 3 — How reasoning was taught before: The standard recipe was supervised fine-tuning on human-written chains: experts write thousands of step-by-step solutions and the model imitates their style. Preference tuning such as InstructGPT (08) then used PPO with a learned reward model, and prompted chain-of-thought asked giant models to think aloud at test time. All three depend on human effort or on a critic nearly as expensive as the policy, and none teaches the model to check its own work.
  • Step 4 — Where it broke, concretely: A human-written chain shows the right path but never shows recovery: if the model takes a wrong turn mid-problem, no example teaches it to catch the mistake. A learned judge can be gamed and needs fresh data, and PPO’s value network struggles when an early sentence looks wrong but the answer is later corrected. Meanwhile the strongest reasoner, o1, was closed and expensive, and small open models were left behind. The field needed verification without hand-written reasoning.

STEP BY STEP · PART 2/3 — THE BELL IN THE MAZE: RULE REWARDS AND GROUP-RELATIVE LEARNING

  • Step 5 — The insight: reward the exit, not the route: Imagine a maze where you never give a child a map. You ring a bell only when they exit correctly, and you require them to narrate the walk inside think tags. Early narrations are short and messy; over many tries the child learns that detours with checking, undoing bad turns, and trying alternatives ring the bell more often. R1-Zero starts from DeepSeek-V3-Base with only that tiny template and learns purely from rule rewards.
  • Step 6 — The minimal prompt and the two rewards: The template is bare: the user’s question, then thinking inside think tags and the final answer after. The reward has two equal halves, each 1 or 0. The accuracy reward comes from a rule checker — boxed math answers matched by sympy, code compiled against hidden tests, multiple-choice matched exactly. The format reward just requires correct think tags. Crucially, no neural reward model exists, so there is nothing to hack and no judge to retrain.
  • Step 7 — GRPO decoded, symbol by symbol: For each question the policy samples 16 answers, each scored by the checker. An answer’s advantage is its score minus the group mean, divided by the group standard deviation — siblings above average are pushed up, the rest down, with no value network needed. A small KL penalty (0.001) keeps the policy near a reference model refreshed every 400 steps. Training used learning rate 3e-6, batches of 512 from 32 unique questions, 10,400 steps, and lengths grown from 32,000 to 65,000 tokens.
  • Step 8 — The base model and inference: None of this changes architecture, so the base matters: DeepSeek-V3-Base is a 671-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts that activates only 37 billion per token, pretrained on 14.8 trillion tokens, with multi-head latent attention for memory-efficient long context and multi-token prediction for faster training. The novelty is entirely in training. At inference you use zero-shot prompting — no few-shot examples, because they degrade reasoning — and let the model emit a long think block followed by a concise answer.
  • Step 9 — Worked mini-example: one question, one group, one update: Take “Solve √(x + 3) = 5”. The policy writes 16 rollouts with boxed answers. The checker marks four correct: twelve 0s and four 1s, mean 0.25, standard deviation ≈0.43, giving correct rollouts advantage ≈ +1.7 and wrong ones ≈ −0.6. STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — WHAT PURE RL PROVED: 15.6 TO 77.9 AND THE WAIT MOMENT

  • Step 10 — The results, and what they mean: R1-Zero alone lifted AIME 2024 pass-at-one from 15.6% for base V3 to 77.9%, and majority voting over 16 samples reached 86.7%, above the human average.   • Step 11 — Why it works: Verifiable rewards cannot be flattered. When the only way to earn the bell is a correct final answer, the model is free to discover any path — and paths that check, backtrack, and verify win more often, so reflection is incentivized rather than taught. GRPO makes this practical because comparing siblings needs no critic when a chain revises itself.   • Step 12 — Honest limits of R1-Zero: Its chains are hard to read, mixing English and Chinese while rambling, which is exactly why a polishing pipeline was needed. Pure RL needs reliable verifiers, so domains without clear checkers — writing, safety, open-ended advice — cannot use Zero directly. It sometimes overthinks simple questions with thousands of tokens, is sensitive to prompts, and few-shot examples hurt. It cannot use tools, browse, or run a calculator while thinking, so all arithmetic happens inside the chain.
  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: R1-Zero proved reasoning can be incentivized rather than imitated, and it parents the polished R1 pipeline and the 800,000-trace distillation that put reasoning into small Qwen and Llama models. It rewrites the deck’s lessons: Chain-of-Thought (09) becomes learned self-verification, InstructGPT (08) PPO becomes GRPO, GPT-3 (03) few-shot becomes zero-shot thinking, and Chinchilla (10) plus FlashAttention (12) make 65,000-token chains affordable. R1-Zero showed that a final-answer reward can produce strong reasoning, but its raw answers were often hard to read and it was not yet a polished, helpful assistant. DeepSeek’s next challenge was to preserve the new reasoning ability while improving clarity, instruction following, language consistency, and safety. The team also wanted to transfer that capability into smaller models that people could run more affordably.

2 · HOW — FOUR STAGES TO THE FINAL ASSISTANT

  1. Cold-start supervised fine-tuning (SFT): The team filters R1-Zero traces for correctness and readability, removes mixed-quality examples, and has annotators rewrite selected answers in a clear first-person style and in the language of the question. This produces Dev1; instruction-following rises from 46 to 72 and Arena from 54 to 77, while AIME temporarily falls from 78 to 59.
  2. Reasoning reinforcement learning (RL): Dev1 is trained on 26,000 mathematics problems, 17,000 algorithm problems, and additional debugging, science, technology, engineering, and logic tasks. A language-consistency reward encourages answers to stay in the language of the prompt. Dev2 recovers reasoning, with Codeforces improving from the 84th to the 90th percentile and AIME returning to about 74.
  3. Rejection sampling and a second SFT round: The team generates many Dev2 answers, keeps 600,000 strong reasoning traces judged by DeepSeek-V3, and adds 200,000 helpful non-reasoning examples. Fine-tuning on this set of 800,000 examples produces Dev3; AlpacaEval rises from 56 to 62 and code-repair performance rises from 26 to 45.
  4. All-scenario RL: The final training stage combines rule-based reasoning rewards with learned helpfulness and safety rewards that score only the answer summary, along with a language-consistency reward. Training uses temperature 0.7 for 1,700 steps, with the last 400 steps focused on general and adversarial prompts.

3 · HOW — DISTILL REASONING INTO SMALL MODELS

The authors use 800,000 polished R1 traces to train six smaller Qwen and Llama models with supervised fine-tuning for two or three epochs and a 32,000-token context window; this stage uses no reinforcement learning. A distilled 32-billion-parameter model scores 72.6 on AIME, compared with 47.0 for the same-size model trained with direct reinforcement learning. The teacher’s worked examples give the smaller model useful reasoning paths without requiring it to discover them through costly trial and error.

5 · WHY DISTILLATION WORKS

Reinforcement learning asks a smaller model to explore many possible reasoning paths, but a capable path is difficult to discover in the limited search space of a 32-billion-parameter student. R1’s verified traces concentrate training on solutions that already work, allowing supervised fine-tuning to teach useful reasoning directly instead of spending rollouts on unsuccessful exploration. At inference, the paper recommends zero-shot prompts because extra examples can reduce reasoning accuracy; the model produces a long reasoning section and then a concise summary.

Reasoning-oriented RL then trains on 26,000 math problems plus 17,000 algorithm, bug-fix, STEM, and logic problems, with a language-consistency reward that counts words in the target language.   • Step 7 — Stages three and four: rejection sampling, then all-scenario RL: Stage three samples Dev2 widely, keeps 600,000 reasoning traces judged correct and clear by V3, adds 200,000 helpful non-reasoning examples, and fine-tunes on the 800,000 total. Stage four runs all-scenario RL combining the rule reward for reasoning, learned helpfulness and safety reward models scoring only the final summary, and the language reward, at temperature 0.7 for 1,700 steps. Final R1 gains 25 points on AlpacaEval and 17 on ArenaHard, with math preserved.
  • Step 8 — Distillation: 800,000 stories for six small students: The same traces then train six small students by supervised fine-tuning alone, two to three epochs at 32,000 context: Qwen2.5-Math 1.5B and 7B, Qwen2.5 14B and 32B, and Llama 8B and 70B. The lesson: distilled 32B scored 72.6 on AIME versus 47.0 for the same model trained with 10,000 RL steps, because small models cannot explore but can imitate a strong teacher. At inference R1 runs zero-shot — few-shot hurts — emitting a long think block then a concise summary.
  • Step 9 — Worked mini-example: one messy trace becomes a lesson: Take “3x + 7 = 22, solve x”. R1-Zero answers with a 12,000-token spiral in mixed languages. Stage one rewrites it as clean first-person steps: 3x = 15, so x = 5. Stage two’s language reward prefers the English version. Stage three keeps it only because the boxed answer 5 is correct and V3 judges it readable, so it joins the 600,000 traces. A 1.5B Qwen student trained on that set reaches 28.9 on AIME and 83.9 on MATH.

STEP BY STEP · PART 3/3 — FRONTIER AT OPEN PRICES: RESULTS, LIMITS, AND THE DISTILLED WAVE

  • Step 10 — The results that matched the frontier: Final R1 scored 79.8 on AIME 2024, tied with o1 at 79.2 and far above o1-mini at 63.6, V3 39.2, and GPT-4o 9.3. It beat o1 on MATH-500 (97.3 versus 96.4) and LiveCodeBench (65.9 versus 63.4), tied it on SWE-Verified (49.2 versus 48.9) and Codeforces (2,029 versus 2,061), and trailed only on GPQA science (71.5 versus 75.7). It also became pleasant: AlpacaEval jumped 55.8 → 87.6, ArenaHard 53.6 → 92.3, and instruction following 46.6 → 83.3, while MMLU stayed at 90.8.
  • Step 11 — Why the design works: Each stage fixes one failure without breaking the others. Cold start buys readability, reasoning RL restores accuracy, rejection sampling broadens into writing and code repair, and all-scenario RL aligns tone and safety. Scoring the learned reward models only on the final summary is the subtle move: the thinker can ramble privately without being punished for style. Distillation then sidesteps exploration entirely — a small model pays one supervised pass over a strong teacher’s 800,000 stories instead of 10,000 expensive RL steps.
  • Step 12 — Honest limits: R1 cannot use tools, browse, or run a calculator while thinking, so it does arithmetic internally and can overthink easy questions with thousands of tokens. Output structure is weak for JSON and strict formats; prompt sensitivity remains and few-shot prompts hurt; English and Chinese mix outside the trained distribution. Safety and writing still need human data and short RL because rule rewards only cover math and code, software-engineering RL was limited by slow evaluation, and long chains cost real latency and money.
  • Step 13 — What it unlocked: R1 shipped free with MIT-licensed weights and an API price near 55 cents per million input tokens, 20 to 50 times below o1 — a shock that on 27 January 2025 cut Nvidia about 17%, wiping ≈$589 billion in a day.
