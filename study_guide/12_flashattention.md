# FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness — Dao, Fu, Ermon, Rudra, Ré, Stanford and Buffalo, 2022 — arXiv:2205.14135

## The story in one paragraph

This paper shows that attention was slow not because it needs too much math, but because it moves too much data between slow and fast memory on the graphics card. The authors design an exact attention algorithm that never creates the giant N-by-N attention matrix in slow memory. Instead it splits queries, keys, and values into small blocks, loads each block into super-fast on-chip SRAM, computes partial attention with careful rescaling, and accumulates the correct answer piece by piece. It also saves only tiny statistics for the backward pass and recomputes what it needs on the fly. The result, called FlashAttention, runs up to seven point six times faster on the attention itself, uses linear instead of quadratic memory, trains BERT-large fifteen percent faster than the MLPerf speed record, trains GPT-2 three times faster, and lets Transformers handle sixteen thousand and even sixty-four thousand tokens for the first time with better-than-chance accuracy.

## What problem was hurting before this paper

Before FlashAttention, standard attention materialized two giant matrices called S and P, where S equals Q times K-transpose and P equals softmax of S. For a sequence of length N, both matrices have N squared entries. With N equal to one thousand and twenty-four, that is already more than a million numbers per head, and with longer texts it quickly explodes. Those matrices had to be written to slow high-bandwidth memory, called HBM, and read back many times for softmax, dropout, masking, and the backward pass. On modern graphics cards, math has become much faster than memory, so most Transformer operations were waiting on memory rather than computing. Many approximate methods tried to reduce math by using sparsity or low-rank tricks, but they still moved a lot of data and often ran no faster in wall-clock time, while also lowering quality. Programmers using PyTorch or TensorFlow could not control the memory movement finely, so even fused kernels still saved giant intermediates for the backward pass.

## The core idea, explained simply (with an analogy)

The core idea is to be IO-aware, which means to count and minimize reads and writes between slow big memory and fast tiny memory, not just to count math operations.

A helpful analogy is cooking in a tiny kitchen with a huge pantry across the street. Standard attention is like carrying every single ingredient across the street, laying them all out on the sidewalk, then carrying them back one by one. FlashAttention is like bringing one small basket of ingredients at a time into the tiny kitchen, cooking that part of the meal completely, writing down only the recipe notes you need, and repeating until the whole feast is done. The kitchen is the on-chip SRAM, which is about nineteen terabytes per second fast but only about twenty megabytes in size on an A100. The pantry across the street is the HBM, which holds forty gigabytes but runs at only about one point five terabytes per second. By never laying the whole feast on the sidewalk, FlashAttention saves enormous walking time, even though it does a little extra chopping by recomputing.

Two old tricks make this possible. Tiling means splitting the work into blocks and doing softmax block by block with rescaling. Recomputation means not storing the giant attention matrix for the backward pass, but quickly rebuilding it from the tiny saved statistics when gradients are needed.

## How it actually works (step by step: method, math explained in words, experiments — explain each piece in words; explain key equations in plain language)

The paper defines attention as S equals Q times K-transpose, P equals softmax of S row by row, and O equals P times V. Here Q, K, and V each have shape N by d, where N is sequence length and d is head size like sixty-four or one hundred and twenty-eight. S and P have shape N by N, which is the problem.

The first building block is online softmax with statistics. For a vector x, normal softmax first finds the maximum m, then computes exponentials shifted by m for stability, then sums them into l, then divides. The clever part is that softmax of two joined blocks can be built from each block alone if you remember each block maximum and sum. In words, the new maximum is simply the larger of the two old maxima. The new sum is the first sum reweighted by how far its maximum is from the new maximum, plus the second sum reweighted similarly. The output is then the correctly reweighted combination. This is sometimes called tiling or algebraic aggregation, and it means you never need the whole row at once.

The FlashAttention forward pass uses this idea in nested loops. It chooses block sizes Bc equals M divided by four d and Br equals the smaller of M divided by four d and d, where M is SRAM size. In words, each block of keys and values must fit comfortably in fast memory together with a block of queries and the running answer. It splits Q into Tr blocks and K and V into Tc blocks. It creates output O filled with zeros and vectors m filled with minus infinity and l filled with zeros in slow memory. Then for each key-value block j, it loads K-j and V-j into SRAM once. Inside, for each query block i, it loads Q-i, the running O-i, l-i, and m-i into SRAM. On chip it computes S-i-j equals tau times Q-i times K-j-transpose, where tau is usually one over the square root of d and helps keep numbers calm. It applies masking, computes the block maximum, the shifted exponentials, and the block sum. It then computes the new maximum and new sum by combining old and new statistics, and it updates the running output with a rescaling formula that says new output equals old output reweighted plus new block contribution reweighted, all divided by the new sum. It writes the updated O-i, l-i, and m-i back to slow memory. After looping over all blocks, the O in slow memory exactly equals softmax of Q times K-transpose times V.

The backward pass is derived with similar care to stay linear in memory. Normally the backward pass would need S and P of size N squared to compute gradients for Q, K, and V. FlashAttention instead saves only O plus the vectors l and m plus the random-number-generator state for dropout. During backward it recomputes each P-i-j on chip from Q-i, K-j, l-i, and m-i, regenerates the same dropout mask from the saved random state, and accumulates gradients for values, queries, and keys block by block. The paper gives explicit formulas. The gradient for values is a weighted sum of output gradients. An intermediate value D-i equals the dot product of output gradient and output, which avoids summing over N. The gradient for scores is P times the difference between the path through values and D-i. The gradients for queries and keys are then weighted sums using those scores. In words, every large sum is broken into small on-chip pieces that are added to running totals, so extra memory stays proportional to N times d rather than N squared.

The IO analysis is the heart of the paper. Standard attention needs Theta of N times d plus N squared slow-memory accesses, because it writes and reads the whole N squared matrix several times. FlashAttention needs Theta of N squared times d squared divided by M slow-memory accesses. In words, if SRAM size M is much bigger than d squared, which is true in practice since d is sixty-four and M is around one hundred kilobytes, FlashAttention moves many times less data, up to nine times less in the paper figure. The authors also prove a lower bound that says no exact attention algorithm can do asymptotically better for all SRAM sizes, so their method is essentially optimal.

The extension to block-sparse FlashAttention simply skips blocks where a block mask M equals zero. If s is the fraction of nonzero blocks, the IO cost becomes Theta of N times d plus N squared times d squared divided by M times s. In words, sparsity gives a direct proportional saving on the big term. For typical patterns like butterfly sparsity, this becomes near N times square-root N or N log N instead of N squared. The paper proves this and shows two to four times extra speed over dense FlashAttention.

Implementation matters a lot. The authors wrote a single fused CUDA kernel that does matrix multiply, masking, softmax, dropout, and second matrix multiply without going back to slow memory in between. Even though recomputation adds extra math, the paper shows with GPT-2 medium at length one thousand and twenty-four that total math rises from sixty-six point six to seventy-five point two gigaFLOPs, yet slow-memory traffic falls from forty point three gigabytes to four point four gigabytes, and runtime falls from forty-one point seven milliseconds to seven point three milliseconds. In plain words, less walking beats a little extra chopping.

## Key results and numbers from the paper (with the actual scores/tables described in words)

On speed, FlashAttention trains BERT-large on Wikipedia with sequence length five hundred and twelve in seventeen point four minutes versus twenty point zero minutes for the Nvidia MLPerf 1.1 record, which is a fifteen percent win to reach seventy-two percent masked-language-model accuracy. On OpenWebText with eight A100 cards, GPT-2 small trains in two point seven days versus nine point five days for HuggingFace and four point seven days for Megatron-LM, which is a three point five times win, with identical perplexity of eighteen point two. GPT-2 medium trains in six point nine days versus twenty-one days for HuggingFace and eleven point five days for Megatron-LM, with perplexity around fourteen point three. On the Long-Range Arena suite with lengths from one thousand to four thousand, FlashAttention averages fifty-nine point eight percent accuracy versus fifty-nine point three percent for standard attention, while running two point four times faster. Block-sparse FlashAttention averages fifty-nine point six percent while running two point eight times faster, beating Linformer, Performer, Reformer, and others on speed and usually on accuracy.

On quality from longer context, GPT-2 small with context four thousand still trains thirty percent faster than Megatron with context one thousand, while improving perplexity from eighteen point two to seventeen point five, which is a zero point seven gain. On long-document classification with RoBERTa, MIMIC-III intensive-care notes improve from fifty-two point eight micro-F1 at length five hundred and twelve to fifty-seven point one at length sixteen thousand, and ECtHR legal cases improve from seventy-two point two to eighty point seven at length eight thousand, which is up to an eight point five point lift.

On Path-X with length sixteen thousand, where every prior Transformer got random chance or ran out of memory, FlashAttention reaches sixty-one point four percent by pre-training on Path-64 and interpolating position embeddings. On Path-256 with length sixty-four thousand, block-sparse FlashAttention reaches sixty-three point one percent, which the authors believe is the first better-than-chance sequence-model result on that task.

On raw benchmarks with eight heads of size sixty-four on one A100, FlashAttention is up to three times faster than PyTorch attention from length one hundred and twenty-eight to two thousand, and it keeps scaling to sixty-four thousand while baselines run out of memory. Memory grows linearly with length, using only about eight hundred and thirty-six megabytes at length four thousand where PyTorch uses about seventeen gigabytes, which is roughly a twenty-times saving. Block-sparse FlashAttention is faster than every approximate and sparse baseline the authors tested, across all lengths.

## Why this paper matters (what later work it unlocked — name the specific later papers among the 15 where relevant)

FlashAttention became the invisible engine inside almost every modern Transformer. The LLaMA-2 paper, which is number thirteen, trains with FlashAttention to handle longer contexts efficiently, and without it the two-trillion-token training would have been far slower and more expensive. The DeepSeek-R1 paper, which is number fifteen, depends on long-context reasoning and efficient attention to train and serve reasoning traces that span many thousands of tokens. The Latent Diffusion paper, which is number seven, and the CLIP paper, which is number six, both use attention heavily in U-Nets and vision Transformers, and FlashAttention-style IO-awareness made high-resolution images and long captions practical. Even alignment work like InstructGPT, which is number eight, and DPO, which is number fourteen, benefits because faster attention means faster sampling of many candidate answers during human-preference training. Beyond our fifteen, FlashAttention led directly to FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3, to native support in PyTorch 2, and to its use in ChatGPT, Claude, and most open models.

## Honest limitations

The authors list three honest limits. First, writing a fast kernel still requires hand-tuned CUDA code for each new attention variant, which takes expert engineering and may not transfer cleanly across graphics-card generations like A100 to H100 to consumer cards. They hope future compilers can turn high-level PyTorch into IO-aware CUDA automatically, like Halide did for images. Second, their optimality proof is for a single graphics card. Large models split attention across many cards, which adds a new memory layer for communication between cards that the paper does not fully solve. Third, FlashAttention itself does not reduce the quadratic amount of math, only the memory traffic and memory size. For extremely long sequences beyond tens of thousands of tokens, you still need sparsity, linearity, or state-space tricks on top, which is why the paper offers block-sparse FlashAttention as a first step rather than a final answer.

## Fun trivia and history (from your web search)

The lead author Tri Dao was a PhD student at Stanford working with Christopher Ré, Stefano Ermon, and Atri Rudra. The key insight came from asking why so many clever linear attentions never felt faster in real life. The answer was that they counted math operations but ignored memory walks, which dominate on modern cards where compute has outrun memory bandwidth. The team started from Nvidia Apex fused multi-head attention code and added tiling plus recomputation, then proved with IO-complexity theory that the result was optimal.

The paper is famous for doing more math yet running faster. Reviewers loved the figure showing gigaFLOPs going up from sixty-six point six to seventy-five point two while runtime falls from forty-one point seven milliseconds to seven point three milliseconds, because slow-memory traffic falls from forty point three gigabytes to four point four gigabytes. That picture taught a generation that FLOPs do not equal speed.

FlashAttention quickly moved from paper to product. It was open-sourced as part of the HazyResearch flash-attention repository, adopted by Megatron-LM, HuggingFace Transformers, and PyTorch, and cited as a reason long-context chatbots became affordable. The Path-X result also has lore. Path-X feeds a one-hundred-and-twenty-eight by one-hundred-and-twenty-eight black-and-white maze one pixel at a time, so the model must remember sixteen thousand pixels to tell if two dots connect. Every Transformer before had failed, and the community had started looking for entirely new architectures, until a faster attention that could simply see farther solved it with sixty-one point four percent.

## Key terms explained (glossary of 5-8 terms)

Self-attention means letting every word look at every earlier word to decide what matters. Its cost normally grows with the square of length, which hurts long documents.

HBM means high-bandwidth memory, which is the large but relatively slow memory on the graphics card, about forty gigabytes at about one point five terabytes per second on an A100.

SRAM means static random-access memory on the chip itself, which is tiny at about twenty megabytes but extremely fast at about nineteen terabytes per second. FlashAttention does its real work here.

Tiling means chopping big matrices into small blocks that fit in SRAM, processing one block at a time, and combining partial answers with correct rescaling.

Recomputation means throwing away big intermediates after the forward pass and quickly rebuilding them during the backward pass, which saves memory traffic even though it repeats some math.

IO-awareness means designing algorithms to minimize data movement, not just math operations. An IO-aware method can be faster even with more FLOPs.

Block-sparse attention means only computing attention for a chosen pattern of blocks and skipping the rest, which gives extra speed proportional to how many blocks you skip.

## Connections (which earlier paper it builds on, which later paper builds on it)

This paper builds directly on the Attention Is All You Need paper, which is number one, because it keeps exact softmax attention mathematically identical while making it IO-efficient. It also connects to the GPT-3 few-shot paper, which is number three, and the BERT paper, which is number two, because those models are the speed benchmarks it beats, and to the RAG paper, which is number four, because retrieval needs long contexts that FlashAttention makes affordable.

Later, the LLaMA-2 paper, which is number thirteen, builds on FlashAttention for efficient training, the Latent Diffusion paper, which is number seven, benefits in its attention-heavy U-Net, and the DeepSeek-R1 paper, which is number fifteen, builds on long-context efficient attention for reasoning over very long traces. The chain-of-thought paper, which is number nine, also becomes more practical because generating many intermediate reasoning steps is much cheaper when each attention step is faster and leaner.
