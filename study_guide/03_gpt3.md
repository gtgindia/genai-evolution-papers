# Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (GPT-3) — Brown, Mann, Ryder, Subbiah, Kaplan, Dhariwal, Neelakantan, Shyam, Sastry, Askell, Agarwal, Herbert-Voss, Krueger, Henighan, Child, Ramesh, Ziegler, Wu, Winter, Hesse, Chen, Sigler, Litwin, Gray, Chess, Clark, Berner, McCandlish, Radford, Sutskever, Amodei, OpenAI, 2020 — arXiv:2005.14165

## The story in one paragraph

This paper asks whether a language model can learn a new task from just a few examples or a simple instruction, with no gradient updates at all, much like humans do. The authors scale a GPT-2 style autoregressive Transformer to one hundred and seventy-five billion parameters, ten times larger than any previous dense model, and train it on about three hundred billion tokens from filtered Common Crawl, WebText, books, and Wikipedia. They then test it in zero-shot, one-shot, and few-shot settings, where tasks are specified purely through text in the context window. They find that larger models use in-context examples more efficiently and sometimes match or beat fine-tuned state-of-the-art systems on translation, question answering, cloze tasks, arithmetic, unscrambling, and analogies, while still struggling on some inference and reading comprehension tasks. They also show that humans can barely distinguish short GPT-3 news articles from human writing, and they discuss contamination, limitations, bias, misuse, and energy use with unusual care.

## What problem was hurting before this paper

Before GPT-3, the dominant recipe was pre-train on large text and then fine-tune on thousands to hundreds of thousands of labeled examples for each new task. This worked well on benchmarks but had three pains. First, collecting a large labeled dataset for every new task is expensive and impractical for the huge range of useful tasks like correcting grammar, generating examples of bravery, or critiquing a story. Second, fine-tuning on a narrow distribution can exploit spurious correlations and generalize poorly out of distribution, so human-level benchmark scores can exaggerate real understanding. Third, humans learn very differently, often from a brief instruction like tell me if this sentence is happy or sad or from one or two demonstrations, and they can fluidly switch tasks mid-conversation, for example doing addition during a dialogue.

Meta-learning and in-context learning offered a way out, where the model learns broad skills during pre-training and then adapts at inference time simply by conditioning on instructions and examples. GPT-2 had shown promising hints, reaching only four percent on Natural Questions and fifty-five F1 on CoQA, far behind fine-tuned systems. The open question was whether scaling capacity, data, and training time would make in-context learning competitive, following smooth power-law trends seen in language modeling loss.

## The core idea, explained simply (with an analogy)

The core idea is that predicting the next word at enormous scale implicitly teaches the model many skills, and those skills can be triggered on demand by showing a few examples in the prompt. You do not update any weights. You simply write the task description and a few solved examples as text, add one unsolved example, and let the model continue the pattern.

A helpful analogy is a brilliant improv actor who has read almost the entire internet. You do not retrain the actor for each new game. You simply say the rules once and act out one or two rounds together, and then the actor instantly plays the next round correctly by recognizing the pattern. Few-shot means you act out ten to one hundred rounds together within the two thousand and forty-eight token memory. One-shot means you show just one round plus instructions, like how crowd workers are taught. Zero-shot means you only give verbal instructions with no examples, which is most convenient but sometimes ambiguous, like asking for a table of two hundred meter records without specifying format. The paper shows that bigger actors get disproportionately better at using those on-stage hints.

## How it actually works (step by step: architecture, training objective, inference — explain each piece in words; if there is a famous equation like scaled dot-product attention, explain what each part means in plain language)

The model and architecture are the same as GPT-2, with modified initialization, pre-normalization, and reversible tokenization, plus alternating dense and locally banded sparse attention patterns like the Sparse Transformer to handle long contexts efficiently. All models use a context window of two thousand and forty-eight tokens. The team trains eight sizes from one hundred and twenty-five million to one hundred and seventy-five billion parameters. The full GPT-3 has ninety-six layers, model width twelve thousand two hundred and eighty-eight, ninety-six heads with head dimension one hundred and twenty-eight, batch size three point two million tokens, and learning rate zero point six times ten to the minus four. Smaller models use proportionally smaller widths, fewer layers, and larger learning rates, as detailed in the paper's table.

The training dataset blends five sources after quality filtering and fuzzy deduplication. Filtered Common Crawl contributes four hundred and ten billion tokens with sixty percent weight but only zero point four four epochs, WebText2 contributes nineteen billion tokens with twenty-two percent weight and two point nine epochs, Books1 contributes twelve billion tokens with eight percent weight and one point nine epochs, Books2 contributes fifty-five billion tokens with eight percent weight and zero point four three epochs, and Wikipedia contributes three billion tokens with three percent weight and three point four epochs. Common Crawl was downloaded from forty-one monthly shards from 2016 to 2019, totaling forty-five terabytes compressed before filtering and five hundred and seventy gigabytes after. Filtering trained a classifier to distinguish high-quality reference text from raw Common Crawl and kept documents probabilistically by score, then removed near-duplicates with MinHash. Sampling deliberately overweights high-quality sets, accepting a little overfitting for better data. Training is on V100 graphics cards with model parallelism across depth and width, Adam with beta one zero point nine, beta two zero point nine five, gradient clipping, cosine decay, warmup, and gradually increasing batch size.

The training objective is standard autoregressive language modeling, which means predicting the next token given all previous tokens. There is no task-specific head during pre-training. Documents shorter than the context window are packed together separated by an end-of-text token, so the model learns that separated contexts are unrelated.

Evaluation defines four settings on a spectrum of task-specific data. Fine-tuning updates weights on thousands of labeled examples and is not done for GPT-3, though possible in principle. Few-shot gives K examples of context plus correct completion, then one context-only query, with K typically ten to one hundred to fit the window. One-shot gives one demonstration plus a natural language description. Zero-shot gives only the description. For multiple choice, the model compares language model likelihood of each completion given the context, often normalized per token for length, and for ARC, OpenBookQA, and RACE also normalized by unconditional probability given a generic answer prefix. For binary classification, options are given meaningful names like True and False. For free-form generation, beam search with beam width four and length penalty zero point six is used, scored with F1, BLEU, or exact match as standard.

## Key results and numbers from the paper (with the actual scores/tables described in words)

On language modeling, GPT-3 sets a new record on Penn Tree Bank with perplexity twenty point five in zero-shot, beating the prior best of thirty-five point eight by fifteen points. Wikipedia-related language sets are skipped because they overlap training data. On LAMBADA long-range prediction, GPT-3 reaches seventy-six point two percent zero-shot versus sixty-eight percent prior best, and eighty-six point four percent few-shot with a fill-in-the-blank framing like Alice was friends with Bob becomes Bob, an eighteen point gain that shows framing matters. On StoryCloze story endings, it reaches eighty-three point two percent zero-shot and eighty-seven point seven percent few-shot with seventy examples, about ten points above prior zero-shot but four points below fine-tuned BERT. On HellaSwag commonsense endings, it reaches seventy-eight point nine percent zero-shot and seventy-nine point three percent few-shot, beating fine-tuned GPT-2 at seventy-five point four but below the multi-task record of eighty-five point six.

On closed-book question answering with no retrieval and no fine-tuning, TriviaQA shines. GPT-3 reaches sixty-four point three percent zero-shot, already fourteen points above fine-tuned T5 eleven billion, sixty-eight percent one-shot matching open-domain retrieval state of the art, and seventy-one point two percent few-shot setting a new closed-book record. On WebQuestions it reaches fourteen point four percent zero-shot, twenty-five point three percent one-shot, and forty-one point five percent few-shot, approaching fine-tuned T5 at thirty-seven to forty-four percent. On Natural Questions it reaches fourteen point six percent zero-shot, twenty-three percent one-shot, and twenty-nine point nine percent few-shot versus thirty-six point six for fine-tuned T5 plus span pre-training, likely because Natural Questions tests fine-grained Wikipedia facts.

On translation with one or sixty-four paired examples in context, zero-shot underperforms prior unsupervised systems, but one-shot gains over seven BLEU and few-shot gains another four BLEU on average. Few-shot reaches thirty-two point six English to French, thirty-nine point two French to English, twenty-nine point seven English to German, forty point six German to English, twenty-one English to Romanian, and thirty-nine point five Romanian to English, beating prior unsupervised work into English but lagging from English, especially English to Romanian. All pairs improve smoothly with size, and into-English is consistently stronger, possibly due to English-heavy byte-level encodings.

On Winograd schemas with partial evaluation, GPT-3 reaches eighty-eight point three percent zero-shot, eighty-nine point seven percent one-shot, and eighty-eight point six percent few-shot on the original two hundred and seventy-three schemas, just below human and fine-tuned records. On adversarial Winogrande it climbs from seventy point two to seventy-three point two to seventy-seven point seven percent, approaching fine-tuned RoBERTa at seventy-nine percent but below the eighty-four point six record. On PIQA physical commonsense it reaches eighty-one zero-shot, eighty point five one-shot, and eighty-two point eight few-shot on the test server, beating the fine-tuned RoBERTa record of seventy-nine point four. On ARC Challenge science exams it reaches about fifty-one to fifty-three percent versus fifty-five point nine for RoBERTa and seventy-eight point five for the multi-task record, and on OpenBookQA it reaches sixty-five point four percent few-shot versus eighty-seven point two record.

On reading comprehension, CoQA conversational F1 reaches eighty-one point five zero-shot, eighty-four one-shot, and eighty-five few-shot, within a few points of human and state of the art at ninety point seven. SQuAD version two improves from fifty-nine point five to sixty-nine point eight F1 from zero to few-shot. DROP discrete reasoning reaches thirty-six point five few-shot, above BERT but far below symbolic hybrids at eighty-nine point one. QuAC dialog and RACE exams lag badly, with RACE high school at forty-six point eight percent versus ninety percent record.

On SuperGLUE with thirty-two examples per task and no weight updates, GPT-3 few-shot scores seventy-one point eight average, beating fine-tuned BERT large at sixty-nine overall and winning on four of eight tasks. It nears state of the art on COPA at ninety-two percent and ReCoRD at ninety point two percent accuracy, reaches eighty point one on WSC, but stays at chance on WiC word-in-context at forty-nine point four percent, revealing weakness on sentence-pair comparison tasks. On adversarial NLI all smaller models sit at chance around thirty-three percent, while GPT-3 shows first signs of life at about forty percent on round three.

Synthetic tasks are striking. On arithmetic with two thousand random problems each, few-shot reaches one hundred percent on two-digit addition, ninety-eight point nine on two-digit subtraction, eighty point four on three-digit addition, ninety-four point two on three-digit subtraction, twenty-five percent on four-digit operations, nine percent on five-digit, twenty-nine point two on two-digit multiplication, and twenty-one point three on composite single-digit expressions, while the thirteen billion model solves two-digit addition only half the time. Only zero point eight percent of addition answers could have been memorized from training data. On word scrambling with one hundred examples, few-shot reaches sixty-seven point two percent on symbol insertion, thirty-seven point nine on cycling letters, thirty-nine point seven and fifteen point one on anagrams, and near zero on reversal, with smooth gains from size. On three hundred and seventy-four SAT analogies, it reaches fifty-three point seven zero-shot, fifty-nine point one one-shot, and sixty-five point two few-shot versus fifty-seven percent average for college applicants.

For news generation with three example articles as conditioning, human accuracy at detecting two hundred word GPT-3 articles is only fifty-two percent, barely above chance, versus eighty-six percent for an intentionally bad control model, with accuracy falling steadily as model size grows. For five hundred word articles, detection remains at fifty-two percent versus eighty-eight percent control. Additional demos show correct use of novel words like gigamuru and plausible grammar corrections from few examples.

## Why this paper matters (what later work it unlocked — name the specific later papers among the 15 where relevant)

GPT-3 proved that scale alone can unlock in-context learning and turned prompting into a core interface for language models. The InstructGPT paper, which is number eight, starts directly from GPT-3 and adds human-feedback fine-tuning to make it follow instructions reliably, which led to ChatGPT. The chain-of-thought paper, which is number nine, shows that prompting the same large decoders to reason step by step dramatically improves math and logic. The Chinchilla paper, which is number ten, revisits GPT-3's training recipe and shows it was undertrained, establishing compute-optimal scaling. The LoRA paper, which is number eleven, makes fine-tuning GPT-3 scale models affordable by training tiny rank updates. The FlashAttention paper, which is number twelve, makes long-context attention for such giants fast and memory efficient. The LLaMA-2 paper, which is number thirteen, distills these lessons into open-weight models. The DPO paper, which is number fourteen, simplifies InstructGPT-style alignment. The DeepSeek-R1 paper, which is number fifteen, pushes reasoning with reinforcement learning on top of large pre-trained decoders. Retrieval-augmented generation, which is number four, CLIP, which is number six, and latent diffusion, which is number seven, all belong to the same scale-and-prompt era that GPT-3 defined.

## Honest limitations

The paper documents clear weaknesses. GPT-3 struggles on natural language inference like ANLI, on comparison tasks like WiC, and on structured reading like QuAC and RACE, where bidirectionality or careful re-reading may matter. Text synthesis still repeats semantically, loses coherence over long passages, contradicts itself, and produces non sequiturs. The model weights every token equally with no notion of importance and no grounding in video or physical interaction. Pre-training sees far more text than any human sees in a lifetime, so sample efficiency is poor. Inference at one hundred and seventy-five billion parameters is expensive and inconvenient, motivating future distillation. Calibration is weak, interpretability is low, and biases from internet data persist. It remains ambiguous whether few-shot truly learns de novo or mostly recognizes patterns seen in pre-training, and a bug in decontamination filtering meant some test overlaps remained, requiring careful clean-subset analysis.

## Fun trivia and history (from your web search)

GPT-3 was published May 28 2020 and quickly became a cultural event when OpenAI opened a private beta API on June 11 2020. Developer Arram Sabeti tweeted that playing with GPT-3 feels like seeing the future, a line widely quoted as capturing the mood. Within weeks people generated tweets, poetry, emails, trivia answers, translations, and even working code with little prompting. Berkeley student Liam Porr famously generated blog posts that were read by twenty-six thousand people with sixty new subscribers before revealing a machine wrote them. The New York Times wrote that GPT-3 can code, blog, and argue, calling it amazing, spooky, humbling, and more than a little terrifying, while MIT Technology Review called it shockingly good and completely mindless.

The paper itself has thirty-one authors and a full contributions page, from infrastructure and sparse Transformers to fairness and threat analysis. API model names hid sizes behind code names ada, babbage, curie, and davinci. Daily Nous hosted nine philosophers on GPT-3, including David Chalmers on general intelligence, later updated with replies written by GPT-3 itself, which remains one of the most charming artifacts of the release. The authors openly admit a bug caused incomplete removal of test overlaps and devote a full section plus appendices to measuring contamination with thirteen-gram matching, concluding most results barely change on clean subsets except flagged cases like PIQA and Winograd.

## Key terms explained (glossary of 5-8 terms)

Autoregressive language model means a model that predicts the next token from all previous tokens, which allows straightforward sampling and likelihood scoring but only looks left during training.

Zero-shot, one-shot, and few-shot learning means specifying a task with no examples, one example, or a few examples in the prompt, with no weight updates, as distinct from fine-tuning which updates weights on thousands of examples.

In-context learning means adaptation that happens inside the forward pass by attending to demonstrations in the context window, where the outer loop of pre-training implicitly learns how to learn from such prompts.

Closed-book question answering means answering factual questions from parameters alone with no retriever or external documents, as opposed to open-book systems that search Wikipedia first.

Data contamination means test examples accidentally appearing in pre-training data, which the paper studies by flagging N-gram overlaps and re-evaluating on clean subsets.

Temperature and top-p sampling means controls for generation randomness, where higher temperature makes output more diverse and top-p restricts sampling to the most likely tokens.

Bias and fairness analysis means probing whether occupations or descriptions skew by gender, race, or religion due to internet-scale stereotypes in training data.

Compute cost in petaflop days means total training computation, where GPT-3 used thousands of petaflop days compared to tens for GPT-2, though generating one hundred pages afterward costs only cents of electricity.

## Connections (which earlier paper it builds on, which later paper builds on it)

GPT-3 builds directly on Attention Is All You Need, which is number one, for the Transformer backbone and sparse attention variants, on BERT, which is number two, as the bidirectional counterpart it contrasts with, and on GPT-2, ELMo, and scaling-laws work for the pre-train then transfer paradigm. It explicitly frames itself against fine-tuning approaches and retrieval-augmented ideas.

Later, InstructGPT, which is number eight, fine-tunes GPT-3 with human preferences. Chain-of-thought, which is number nine, prompts it to reason aloud. Chinchilla, which is number ten, corrects its compute tradeoffs. LoRA, which is number eleven, FlashAttention, which is number twelve, LLaMA-2, which is number thirteen, DPO, which is number fourteen, and DeepSeek-R1, which is number fifteen, all scale, accelerate, share, align, or teach reasoning to GPT-3 style autoregressive Transformers.
